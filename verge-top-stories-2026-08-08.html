Nitecore’s latest power bank is the lightest and most compact yet

Read on theverge.com

There’s two things you should know about me, your intrepid reviewer: I hate the feature creep associated with modern power banks, and I love shaving grams off the gear I carry when backpacking,  bikepacking, and trail running. So imagine my delight when Nitecore released a new generation of its ultralight NB10000 battery. After a few weeks with it I can confidently say: Haribo be damned!

The $83.95 NB10000 Gen 4 weighs just 146 grams, according to my scale, and measures 1.45 x 11.6 x 4.68cm (0.57 x 4.57 x 1.84 inches), according to Nitecore’s spec sheet. That makes it the lightest and most compact 10,000mAh power bank I can find. Even when factoring in the extra 6 grams added by the included USB-C lanyard, it still beats the previous ultralight champ: Nitecore’s NB10000 Gen 3. The secret to the Gen 4’s impressive weight is a silicon-carbon anode, the same battery tech increasingly appearing in flagship smartphones, including Samsung’s.

As small as it is, the Gen 4’s 39Wh battery still fully recharged my aging iPhone 15 Pro twice, with a few watt-hours left in the tank. And with up to 22.5W of output, it can even top-up my 14-inch MacBook Pro when needed. It also supports pass-through to charge your gadgets as the power bank itself charges. 

Have I found power bank perfection? Almost.

I like that the NB10000 Gen 4 gets all the basics right. It has a simple, four-element indicator to show the battery level, which I much prefer over a fancy display. It’s constructed out of carbon fiber sheets and offers IPX7 protection against water ingress, allowing it to withstand rain or even temporary submersion – exactly what you want from an adventure battery.

In my testing, I measured 33.58Wh of the claimed 39Wh capacity for an 86.1 percent efficiency. That’s a pretty good result for a premium power bank and a slight improvement on the 33.33Wh we measured on the Gen 3 model. That works out to 229.98Wh/kg of delivered energy, or 220.90Wh/kg with the cable — class-leading results, either way. The NB10000 Gen 4 supports PD, QC3.0, and Huawei’s SCP charging protocols.

The Gen 4 model also didn’t suffer from the overheating and shutdown issues we saw when the Gen 3 would run low on juice. I did, however, see a temporary interruption in power delivery when the Gen 4 unit was almost fully depleted, but that’s only because I had a meter attached — otherwise I wouldn’t have known since it continued charging almost immediately. 

The only thing that could be considered superfluous is the animated RGB lighting that flows around the dual USB-C ports. The lights are there to guide you to the USB-C ports in a dark tent, says Nitecore, but you can also… feel them. Give the battery a double-knock near the top and the lights (and battery indicator) illuminate. This can be helpful if wearing gloves since Nitecore inexplicably opted for a touch-sensitive wake button on the Gen 4. Fortunately, that so-called “Touch Button” did reliably sense my wet finger in testing. The knock-to-wake feature seemed to only work about 75 percent of the time, but it, and the RGB lights, can be disabled if you want.

The lights will also glow yellow (regular mode) or green (eco) after a long press of the power button if you want to toggle between full power and extended life, respectively. Eco mode works by dropping the output to a slow 5W (5V at 1A) max. This can keep the power bank from automatically shutting off when charging low-draw devices like headlamps, watches, or wireless earbuds. It can also yield a little more total charge capacity by producing less heat waste, and allow the battery to operate closer to its native voltage resulting in less conversion loss.

The Nitecore NB10000 Gen 4 is the ideal power bank for endurance athletes, backpackers, or anyone looking for an ultralight and ultracompact power bank that mostly avoids feature creep. The only real negative is the price, having gone up to $83.95 since the launch of the $64.95 Gen 3 model two years ago. That makes the Gen 4 power bank two to three times more expensive than the typical 10,000mAh power bank, but it can save you 50 grams or more. That’s a price premium I can see lots of active, outdoorsy people happily paying for.

Is this $450 laptop from an unknown brand too good to be true?

Read on theverge.com

Finding a good laptop under $500 was hard enough before RAMageddon. They nearly always had cheap hardware and underpowered, often outdated chips. That’s what made the MacBook Neo so disruptive: It offered great build quality and good-enough performance starting at $599. Windows laptops couldn’t touch it. Even though it’s gone up to $699, it’s still the laptop to beat.

So when little-known Chinese brand Chuwi announced a $450 Windows laptop with an aluminum build and a brand-new Intel Wildcat Lake processor, it sounded too good to be true.

It is.

Chuwi UniBook

The UniBook comes in one configuration: a 14-inch with an Intel Core 3 304 processor, 8GB of soldered RAM, and a user-swappable 256GB SSD. That’s the lowest-end of Intel’s new Wildcat Lake chips, with just five CPU cores: one performance core, and four low-power efficiency cores. And the integrated graphics? A single GPU core. The MacBook Neo’s A18 Pro is a six-core CPU / five-core GPU ensemble, and it’s a hot rod by comparison.

  • Webcam: D
  • Keyboard: C

The UniBook feels sluggish in anything beyond basic web browsing, emails, and messaging. Its benchmark scores confirm how woefully underpowered that chip is. The MacBook Neo trounces it across the board: both single and multicore CPU performance, as well as graphics. Our Adobe Premiere 4K export test took over an hour on the UniBook. (Although at least it didn’t crash out.) I know it isn’t designed for video editing, but neither is the MacBook Neo, and the Neo exports the same video in under 10 minutes. The one edge the UniBook has is a slightly faster SSD.

But despite faster storage, the UniBook’s 8GB of RAM is still a hindrance on Windows 11. It didn’t randomly lag like the recent 13-inch Surface Laptop did, but it isn’t hard to overwhelm. As on the 8GB Surface, Windows 11 uses anywhere from 4.6 to over 5GB of memory on the UniBook after a fresh reboot with no additional apps running. Once you open a half dozen Chrome tabs, a couple messaging apps, and a music streaming app, memory usage easily climbs to 7GB. 

It’s no surprise Chrome crashed on me a couple times when I pushed it further with over 20 tabs across multiple virtual desktops. This is no problem on Windows laptops with 16GB of RAM, and even the MacBook Neo could handle more before slowing down. And you can count on everything moving like molasses during big file transfers. It couldn’t even load the Start menu or open the calculator app while importing photos into Lightroom Classic until the RAM and SSD bottlenecks eased. 

Even if your computing needs are minimal, the Chuwi’s hardware makes every interaction unpleasant. The screen is washed out, covering just 64 percent of the sRGB color space, and at 300 nits it’s pretty dim. But the worst offender is the trackpad. It’s incredibly stiff to click. You have to press it so hard the whole chassis flexes a little under the pressure. You don’t have to click the trackpad; it can detect taps too, but I never like the loosey-goosey feel of tapping — especially when executing right-clicks or click-and-drags. Using this trackpad made me physically and mentally tired. And clicking around on a laptop? Pretty important!

The speakers are an affront to any music you play, as well as anyone else within earshot. They’re a tinny mess. The phone in your pocket has fuller sound. The 720p webcam is only a smidge better, but it’s still offensive to your face (and possibly your self-confidence). It’s almost passable in the best lighting, but it’s low-res, with a green color cast. In dim or backlit lighting you become a dark blur. After a few days, colleagues started greeting me in meetings with, “Oh, you must be on the Chuwi.” It’s like being told “Wow, you look tired.”

“Uhh, yeah. Nice to see you too.” 

Amid all these downsides, there are a few bright spots. The UniBook does at least have good battery life. It easily lasts up to 10 hours on battery while doing the lightest of work: writing emails, casually browsing the web, and opening the occasional PDF. And the keyboard isn’t bad. It’s backlit, which is a genuine advantage over the MacBook Neo. It’s fine to type on, but the Neo’s and many other Windows keyboards out there feel less spongy. Also, a lack of function lock means adjusting brightness, volume, or media playback always requires a two-key combo. Not a huge deal, but a strange omission of a usual mainstay.

The one thing about the UniBook that genuinely surprised me, aside from the initial reverse sticker shock, was its amount of ports. This little thing has five total USB ports, HDMI-out, microSD, and even an ethernet jack. You might expect all that on a massive gaming laptop, not a thin-and-light. But sadly, none of the USBs are faster than 5Gbps, and the HDMI is just 1.4b — topping out at a stuttery 30Hz at 4K.

Any money spent on peripherals to offset the Chuwi’s shortcomings would be better spent on a different laptop. I’ve started testing a $500-ish HP OmniBook 3, and coming from the Chuwi, it was a huge relief. Even its cheap-as-they-get Snapdragon X chip offers eight cores, buoyed by twice the RAM and storage of the Chuwi, and that processor already proved it had exceptional battery life when I reviewed it in the Lenovo IdeaPad Slim 3x. 

I wasn’t overly impressed with the Acer Aspire 14 AI, which you can often get around $600, when I pitted it and the IdeaPad against the MacBook Neo, but it’s way better than the Chuwi for $155 more. Its Intel Lunar Lake chip gets good battery life and is powerful enough to play indie games and last-gen 3D titles on ultra-low settings. It has twice the RAM and storage as the Chuwi and nearly as many ports, and the Acer’s ports are much faster.

I take no joy in telling you the Chuwi UniBook is bad and frustrating to use. I want to see decent cheap laptops, and I’m all for them coming from lesser-known brands. But Chuwi’s upstart status also brings long-term customer support into question — it doesn’t seem to have US customer service and repair centers like bigger laptop makers. And in early 2026, the company was found to have shipped laptops and mini PCs with older AMD chips disguised as newer, faster ones (even at the BIOS level). Chuwi eventually put out a statement blaming a “production error” and offered refunds for a limited time, but the situation does not inspire confidence.

The UniBook doesn’t leave a great first impression, but I want to remain hopeful that better things are on the horizon — if not for Chuwi, then at least for the Wildcat Lake processor family. Most of the major laptop makers are launching budget laptops with higher-end Wildcat Lake chips to try to compete with the Neo. Microsoft also claims it’s working on improving Windows 11’s performance with 8GB of RAM — which these new laptops desperately need.

With laptop prices skyrocketing due to the RAM and storage crunch, we could all use a win. But the UniBook isn’t it.

Musician and entrepreneur Tom Vek is building a digital music player, but don’t call it retro

Read on theverge.com

Tom Vek burst onto the scene in 2005 with his album We Have Sound, which garnered a solid 7.6 from the tastemakers of the day over at Pitchfork. His undeniably catchy brand of dancy indietronica landed him an appearance on The OC and placement on the Grand Theft Auto IV soundtrack. His follow-up, Leisure Seizure, had a lengthy gestation period, but arrived in 2011 to similar success, reaching No. 79 on the UK charts.

View Link

Vek has continued to release music at regular intervals over the last 15 years, dropping Newer Symbols in 2022. But in 2020 he started toying with this idea he had for a music player called Sleevenote. Inspired by vinyl packaging, it was his way of trying to bring some of the beauty and magic of cover art and liner notes to your digital audio. Securing parts, like square screens, proved difficult. Plus, the original prototype was enormous — roughly the size of a 7-inch single, just thicker and heavier.

Now Vek is back with a new version of Sleevenote that’s smaller, cheaper, and, for a little while longer, available to purchase. The last handful of the Day One Editions are available now for $349. As he turns his attention to the next chapter for his media player passion project, he told us about the essentials, his obsessions, and pet peeves as a musician-turned-entrepreneur.

What is your most indispensable tool (analog or digital, app or hardware)?

As much as I’d love to say my GMT wristwatch, because of all the communication we do with our friends in Seattle, and my love of wristwatches, the real answer has to be a MacBook. I’m either designing UI, designing graphics, retouching photos, or making videos, discussing ideas, implementing ideas, or hearing how my ideas go down. It truly is the motorbike for my mind. I have to see ideas to know whether I like them or not. I’m very fickle regarding industrial design of the tech I use and a big fan of the current “M” MacBooks, Apple kinda lost it for a bit.

How many tabs do you have open right now?

I get a lot of shit from Sleevenote co-founder Chris for how many tabs I have open, also in multiple Chrome profiles, you know, to the point the tabs get so narrow that you have to remember what it is from the favicon. I like coming back to things, as well as having a selection of regularly used things in a row. I find it useful for remembering all the social apps I’m meant to be posting my latest Sleevenote graphic to.

Which tech trend do you wish would go away?

This is a bit salty, but the trend for new retro-style CD players and cassette players. It is a great sign that people want offline dedicated music experiences, but I want to see new ideas. I’m specifically interested in ideas that push for digital music purchasing because it’s far better for musicians than buying that second-hand CD. I want my kids’ generation not to be stuck in a retro cycle, or a doomed big tech cycle. I want to see thoughtfully created indie tech products that could not have existed at any other time. Plus, I remember cassettes, they sound awful. 

What is one thing you wish you had created?

I will answer this with a song: three years after my debut album, when I should have had my second album together, I heard “Jump In The Pool” by Friendly Fires, featuring one of the most stunning synth solos of all time. I would have loved to have created that song at that time. Especially since I’d pushed myself and my then manager fully-clothed into a pool around that time and hadn’t even thought to write a song about it.

What is your current obsession?
Been an obsession for quite a while but it’s still: carports in architecture. I’m a car guy, I’m a very visually-focused car guy. I’m not really fussed about performance or track racing or anything. My real love is the design of the automobile and how it’s an emotive expression of movement. The perfect abode to me has car(s) on show, not hidden away in a garage, but still protected, so the carport becomes this perfect idea. I’m obsessed with mid-century desert architecture which incorporated the automobile so well into the language of the house, but even in the rainy UK, being able to get in and out of your car without getting wet would just make me so happy, I think haha.

What’s the last piece of physical media you bought?
I buy lots of fiction books. I love a small paperback. I just picked up a book called The Cowards based purely on the cover. I’ve just finished a load of Steinbeck, so looking for something else. It’s funny that I often use the shortest elevator pitch “Sleevenote is a Kindle for music”, but I’ve never liked the idea of a digital book. Most people read one book at a time, but having all your music accessible at any one time is much more of a reason for a digitisation of a collection, it seems to me.

What do you think is worth splurging on?
Digital music downloads are worth splurging on. We’ve been led to believe there’s no point in buying anything digitally, but it’s standing in plain sight as the best thing to do with things like music. Not only does it send far greater support for artists, but it gives us music fans offline and time-stamped access to great art, and being encouraged to spend some time with a curated selection of art, we all know is good for the mind and soul. I want Sleevenote to enable this.

What would the tagline for your biopic be?

Encompassing all my work, I suspect it would be “Nothing But Green Lights”, the name of one of my songs, which I’d also apply to my work with other projects. It’s good, not always easy, to try to think there’s nothing in the way of going where you want to go.

Buc-ee’s dodges John Oliver to sue another small business

Read on theverge.com

Buc-ee’s became something of a viral sensation during the World Cup, but it has a troubling history of suing small gas stations and convenience stores. On a recent episode of Last Week Tonight, John Oliver literally begged the company to sue him for selling merch featuring his squirrel mascot, Mr. Nutterbutter, with branding that reads “Buc-Off.” But Buc-ee’s has so far declined to take the bait, instead targeting yet another small business that lacks the deep pockets and legal team of HBO.

According to WDTN, an NBC affiliate in Miami Valley, Ohio, Buc-ee’s opened its first locations in the state earlier this year. And now it’s targeting established local businesses through trademark suits. Beaver’s Mini Mart in Beavercreek has been a staple of the city for “decades.” But Buc-ee’s is claiming that this long-running store’s beaver logo could confuse customers. WDTN says:

In the suit, filed days ago, Buc-ee’s alleges that the Mini Mart’s cartoon beaver mascot is too similar to their own, with its “wide eyes and a smile” that also “uses red as a predominant color,” and could cause confusion.

Oliver and Mr. Nutterbutter have already fended off one lawsuit, with Bob Murray, a mining executive, having unsuccessfully sued for defamation following a 2017 episode about the coal industry.
