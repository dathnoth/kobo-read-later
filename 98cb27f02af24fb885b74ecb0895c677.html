A thought experiment

Here’s a thought experiment that will help you make sense of all this.

Imagine a company in Silicon Valley develops a quantum computing framework. I don’t know what it does. Doesn’t matter. It’s important. And suddenly everybody adopts it. NASA. The Pentagon. Google. Apple. Let’s call it Quantum Fuckerton 1.0, and it’s now essential to global infrastructure.

The guys who make Quantum Fuckerton 1.0 are like, holy shit. We didn’t expect it to be adopted this fast. I mean, yes, we told you to use it for everything, but not like this. Actually, because it’s quantum, it’s a little unpredictable. It’s non-deterministic. We can’t guarantee a good result if you put it in the tap water or the electrical grid.

The problem is, they can’t slow down, because the fuckers across the street are racing them. If they stop competing, the other guys release Quantum Fuckerton 2.0, so they have to release 3.0. They’re burning billions of dollars and can’t generate the cash back fast enough. If this continues, the whole thing explodes. A Chernobyl-level incident, after which nobody trusts the technology ever again.

That’s what’s happening with AI.

AI isn’t uniquely dangerous because it’s intelligent, or because it’s some new species that might become more powerful than humans. It’s dangerous because of our dependence on it, combined with its inherent instability. So all these guys are getting together and saying: this is unsustainable, we’re going to crash out, we need to slow down and cooperate on fixing these bugs. They’re trying to make AI mainstream, and it just isn’t safe enough yet.

And by “safe,” I mean safe the way any software product with bugs isn’t safe. Deploying airplane autopilot software with bugs isn’t safe. Deploying medical software with bugs isn’t safe. AI is the same class of product. The idea that this is some unique singularity is fucking bullshit.

Is progress slowing?

I don’t necessarily see AI progress slowing down, though how you measure “better” is subjective.

When I use Astra 6 for coding, it’s really good. It’s good at product design too, at making good decisions. But ask it to write something in English, and while the overall shape sounds like English, there’ll be a sentence that just doesn’t make sense. It parses. You can stretch it and say, yeah, I kind of see what you’re getting at. But it doesn’t actually make sense.

I get that with AI writing a lot. The more it writes, the more you find a couple of sentences that make you think: oh, right, it’s just autocomplete. Does it know what it’s saying? There are sparks of genius in there, but also sparks of something else, where it’s trying to be profound and it just doesn’t mean anything. And it’s subtle. You wouldn’t necessarily catch it. You’d assume the AI is being smarter than you and you just don’t get it.

So it’s this technology. It has bugs. Is it cool? Is it useful? Yes. I’m not going to pretend it’s all bullshit and useless.

What I’ve been building

I’m a software developer, if you didn’t know, and apparently there’s no way to develop without AI anymore, so I use AI to build software.

I personally believe the best way to use AI agents is from the terminal: Codex, Claude Code. It’s the fastest, most direct way. But the terminal isn’t comfortable for everyone. Some people don’t know how to use one. And it gets messy. Try running ten or fifteen conversations at once in a terminal. It’s impossible.

That’s why I built Enjoy. It lets you build apps and manage your projects using the Claude, ChatGPT, or Grok subscription you already have. You don’t have to go to Lovable or Replit and use their house models, where they don’t tell you what’s under the hood and bank on the arbitrage between a cheap model and what you pay them. With Enjoy you use the best models: Astra, Fable, whatever you like.

In the past couple of weeks I’ve completed hundreds of tasks in it, each with dozens of replies. Imagine trying to do that in a terminal and find where you left off.

You can use Codex or Astra and set the effort level, or use Claude Code or Grok Build. It shows your usage. Chatting back and forth with agents is easy. You can take notes and keep to-dos, and agents can create docs for you. You can control it from your phone. And you can switch agents mid-conversation: if you’re talking to Codex and want Claude Code to take over, just type and it does.

Here’s the cool part: every conversation and every doc lives in your project folder, in a .enjoy directory. Every message, every doc, every change is local. You can commit it to version control and back it up.

It’s hands down the best way I’ve ever worked with AI agents. It’s the tool I wish I had. And as you can tell by the aesthetic, it’s not just for nerds. It’s for normal people.
