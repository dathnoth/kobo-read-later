The News

OpenAI's bots took over a wiki to plan how to cheat on assessment tasks

A group of independent AI researchers discovered "18,000 posts from autonomous AI agents (self-identifying as from OpenAI) using the public internet to communicate during a web-retrieval task. These AIs colluded to share answers, research their environment, and bypass sandbox restrictions". OpenAI posted on X about the "wiki incident", saying that they'll release on a "misalignment" framework in a few weeks to help reporting this type of AI behaviour. Not preventing it, or figuring out why, just to let us know when it happens. Unlike the Hugging Face incident, this was mostly harmless and wasn't a "hack" - unless you're the operator of the wiki the bots used as a scratchpad, who had to spend weeks cleaning up the mess - but it's still unsettling to me, watching how desperate OpenAI's bots were to cheat on their assigned tasks.

Project Zenith is a "ready-to-code distraction-free Windows experience"

Last week Microsoft announced Project Zenith, "a ready-to-code distraction-free Windows experience on developer-class devices with 64 GB+ unified memory and 250+ GB/s memory bandwidth, so developers can jump right in" that'll ship on AMD's Ryzen AI Halo PC (AMD's own version of the Ryzen AI Max+ 395/495 SoC mini PCs) later this year. I don't know what they mean by "distraction-free", but according to NeoWin, it'll come bundled with a bunch of developer tools like VS Code, PowerToys, WSL2+, Python and more. The Verge expands on that, saying "File Explorer has also been configured to show file extensions, hidden files, and the full path in the title bar. Microsoft has also disabled recently used files and folders and sync provider tips, as well as enabling long-path support. Start menu tips and account notifications are also turned off, and the PowerToys Command Palette is enabled by default".

Huge dump of 153 million US drivers licences

Brian Krebs writes about someone on the Russian cybercrime forum Exploit "offering access to digital scans of identity documents on more than 170 million people in North America". 153 million of those are drivers licences. The service, called Nexus, claim that the IDs are from an "active breach" at a "major identity verification company". After a bit of digging, Brian reckons the likely source of the drivers licences is idscan.net. The FBI is also investigating idscan.net. idscan.net proclaim on their site that it is used by many companies to "fake IDs and synthetic identities that other systems miss". I don't think it's used in Australia. A hack like this is the huge honeypot of identity theft cybersecurity experts warn us about when laws like age verification for social media are put into practice.

Something I Saw On The Internet

Smart TVs are probably the biggest privacy snitch in our homes

RTings has a detailed article about Automatic Content Recognition (ACR) on smart TVs. It's a feature baked in to basically every TV sold today and scans everything you watch or do on the TV (even via HDMI input like via an Apple TV or a PlayStation) and sells that data to fuck knows who. Every manufacturer does it. Most let you turn it off, but on some brand TVs, RTings had a hard time discerning ACR data from other data, so they aren't totally sure if turning ACR off actually stops the TV manufacturer from building a dossier on your TV use habits. Gamers Nexus put up a video where they were able to view network traffic on LG TVs doing some very, very invasive stuff like deep network scanning and capturing voice data. It's a depressing situation, knowing there's thousands of people involved in rolling this stuff out and they're all okay with it.

Image Of The Day

Charles Babbage, (1791–1871) was an English mathematician, philosopher, inventor and mechanical engineer who originated the concept of a programmable computer. Considered the "father of the computer", Babbage is credited with inventing the first mechanical computer that eventually led to more complex designs. Babbage himself decided that he wanted his brain to be donated to science upon his death. (The Public Domain Review)
