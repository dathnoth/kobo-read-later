Sony pushes forward with ditching discs, despite backlash

Read on theverge.com

Sony has received a lot of backlash from PlayStation fans since announcing that it’s killing physical game disc production, but that hasn’t swayed its decision. During Sony’s latest earnings call, chief financial officer Lin Tao said that while the company “put in a lot of thought and time” when considering the opposition put forward by its gaming community, Sony has come to the conclusion that it will “cautiously move this forward.”

“There are various reasons we made this decision, the biggest being that the digitalization of content overall has been progressing, that’s the big factor,” Tao said through an interpreter. “It’s not just for PlayStation, but for all kinds of content, digitalization is progressing.”

Sony’s plan to cease production of physical discs for new PlayStation games by January 2028 was announced earlier this month, and the company has already begun repurposing its disc-manufacturing headquarters to work on optical microlenses instead. The decision to limit gamers to digitally downloaded games has since been loudly criticized by some PlayStation console users who feel it will negatively affect resale markets, true game ownership, and the preservation of gaming media. A weeklong blackout for PlayStation content has been organized in protest of the decision, which is set to start on August 23rd.

Sony’s claims around the ongoing digitalization of content do hold merit, however. Its latest quarterly earnings report shows that physical games earned the company just over $781 million in the 2025 fiscal year — a fraction of what it brought in for digital game sales (about $6.5 billion) and in-game purchases (about $8.4 billion) over the same period.

Apple TV’s Cape Fear remake turns it into a paranoid tech thriller

Read on theverge.com

Cape Fear has always been a thriller about paranoia. And in 2026 there are few things less trustworthy than technology.

Apple TV’s recent remake of Cape Fear is “inspired” by the 1991 film of the same name (Martin Scorsese and Steven Spielberg even returned as executive producers on the project), which itself is a remake of a 1962 movie that was an adaptation of the novel The Executioners by John D. MacDonald. The series is essentially a brand-new story within a similar premise, and it makes a number of significant changes to make it both fit the new episodic format and feel right at home in the present day. One of the most salient updates is how it utilizes various forms of technology to make its characters — and audience — feel increasingly destabilized. 

In the just-wrapped 10-episode season, convicted murderer Max Cady (Javier Bardem) is released 17 years into a life sentence after someone else confesses to the crime. And when Cady is finally free, he puts all of his energy into making the lives of the lawyers from his trial — defense attorney Anna Bowden (Amy Adams) and prosecutor Tom Bowden (Patrick Wilson), who have since married and had children — into a living hell. Cady describes his prison experience as “death by a thousand cuts,” and he goes about putting the Bowdens through a similar experience of escalating terror and violence.

It starts out small and decidedly low-tech when the Bowdens find a family of skunks — two parents, two babies — dead in their pool. But as things intensify, Cady quickly adds various forms of tech to his arsenal of psychological weapons. In order to get close to Anna’s son Zack (Joe Anders), Cady and his conspirators catfish him both through text messages and chats in a World of Warcraft-style online game. Given how tech is so tightly woven into our daily lives, it becomes a perfect venue for making the Bowdens feel increasingly scared and on edge, which fits right into Cady’s plan.

As the show progresses, Cady utilizes a range of other gadgets and tech to create the sense of foreboding and terror he’s looking for. Drones ominously fly over the Bowden house as their daughter Anna (Lily Collias) does yoga, Tom gets in hot water at his office because of what appears to be an AI clone of his voice, and their home security cameras always seem to glitch out at just the wrong time. These scenes can be creepy in the moment, but they often also have a lasting impact. The footage of Anna, for instance, is made public in an incredibly awkward way, further isolating her from her friends.

The best thing about this version of Cape Fear is how it manages to steadily ratchet up the tension, constantly making viewers, alongside the Bowden family, wonder just what’s real. This isn’t achieved solely through technology. There are plenty of more traditional ways that Cady terrorizes his prey: stalking to the point that he buys a house across the street, getting uncomfortably close with the children, and even some good old-fashioned brainwashing related to a woefully underexplained pseudo-religion. You also can’t discount the sheer presence of Javier Bardem, who imbues the character with an appropriate level of charming chaotic energy, which makes things feel all the more intense. This is a guy who makes eating an ice cream cone seem scary.

Using tech as a quick and easy way of updating a story isn’t a new concept by any means. It can be a simple shorthand for showing when a show takes place; even the new season of King of the Hill has an episode where Hank gets scammed by an AI clone of his son’s voice. But what Cape Fear does so wisely is understand how reliant we are on technology now, and then uses that to add even more uncertainty to its story. In the world of Cape Fear, you can’t trust anything: the messages on your phone, in-game chats with your friends, the cameras protecting your home, even your own voice. And it comes at a time when many of us are becoming more and more distrustful of certain technologies in the real world.

Cape Fear isn’t simply adding modern references to make its story feel more timely. It’s using the trappings of our present day to amplify its disturbing nature, which is something that continues throughout the entire show. Max Cady is the kind of character who will use any tool at his disposal, and in 2026 there are just so many more tools. As Cape Fear approached its finale, major revelations came from a hidden USB drive full of photos, a secret recording on a stolen iPhone, and a screen recording of a FaceTime call. The inclusion of so much technology makes Cape Fear feel like a modern story — but it’s also what gives this version its terrifying new edge.

This tattoo is permanent, pain-free, and might soon come in the mail

Read on theverge.com

The refurbished railway station in London I entered last week had none of the usual signs of being a tattoo studio. The walls were devoid of any art, and there were no reclining chairs or massage tables in the room. The only tattoo gun on the premises sat in a display case instead of buzzing in someone’s gloved hand. I felt like I was getting inked in a half-assembled biolab with some interesting benefits.

I’m not trying to put on a show of bravado when I say my latest tattoo didn’t hurt. Getting the small sparkle lineart permanently marked on my skin was a genuinely painless experience. It was over in seconds, and didn’t involve any grisly needles — at least, not in the traditional sense.

It was applied by CipherX, a London-based biotech company that’s developed disposable “Microdot” patches that provide an alternative form of permanent tattooing. Each patch is roughly the size of a US quarter and uses microscopic, biodegradable needles to deposit pigment into the upper layers of the skin. The process is designed to be “quiet, fast, and pain-free,” all of which I found to be true, opening the door for even more people to rock some permanent ink.

Some cosmetic products already use a similar technology, and microneedle tattoo patches have been explored since at least 2022, but CipherX appears to be the first to turn that latter concept into a consumer-ready product. My Verge colleagues have previously put their bodies on the line in the name of testing technology, and I was more than happy to offer up my flesh.

The application itself was actually faster than I expected. After choosing my design — a “Bold Sparkle,” now widely associated with AI iconography — CipherX CEO Ferdinand Kohle placed an ink-coated transparent guide disc on my arm to indicate where it would be applied. He then lined up the seemingly 3D-printed applicator with the ink guidelines, pressed a button, and removed it to reveal the Microdot patch affixed to my arm, embedding 112 microneedles into my skin.

A flexible tattoo bandage was applied to prevent it from moving around, and a branded plastic sticker was placed over that — presumably for marketing as Kohle admitted that part wasn’t necessary. The final step involved waiting 15 minutes for it to process, and I was free to move around during that time.

I was warned that I might feel a slight “flicking” sensation as the applicator was pressed, but I barely noticed a thing. It felt more like someone had lightly touched my arm with their finger. I wouldn’t even describe the sensation as uncomfortable, let alone painful. It’s also worth mentioning that I have sensitive skin and bleed very easily, but I didn’t experience any bleeding or irritation from the microneedles. There was no scabbing or healing time at all, unlike my other tattoos. 

The tattoo is revealed after the patch is removed, and becomes visibly darker over the course of two days. Initially, the tattoo consisted of lots of teeny dots rather than a smooth, connected line. The ink is supposed to “bleed” together as it develops, like a regular tattoo does over the years, but that hasn’t occurred in the five days since I had it done. I now have to look very closely to see the dots, but they’re definitely still there.

The superfluous branded sticker they applied over the tattoo bandage was actually the most annoying part of the entire experience because it pinched my skin when I moved my arm. I had the tattoo applied near the inside of my elbow as that’s one of the more painful areas for traditional tattooing — I have my ankles done already (another notable pain zone) and wanted to compare. 

This isn’t a viable solution for tattooing most of the more painful body areas like your ankle joint or fingers though. The applicator requires enough of a flat surface area to accommodate the patch and skin that can be lightly stretched, so bony areas aren’t ideal if you want every bit of the tattoo to come out cleanly. CipherX also isn’t applying these on people’s face, neck, hands, or groin area, but that’s more because the company isn’t comfortable doing so, and some tattoo artists reject those areas over similar personal boundaries.

Right now, the tattoo selection only includes about 15 designs, they can’t be customized or measure larger than 15mm in diameter, and black is the only color option. CipherX is working on expanding beyond those limitations: It’s developing additional colors, including an invisible UV-reactive ink, and is working on a larger size option, both of which were previewed at the event. The company is also increasing its range of designs and plans to collaborate with established artists for future releases.

As someone who prefers larger, colorful custom ink, I can confidently say that I’m not the target demographic for Microdot tattoos, but I’m also not entirely sure who is. The tats could be a good space filler for people with existing tattoos, but those folks clearly don’t have any issues with going the more painful traditional route. It’s a good option for needlephobes, but they would have to really want a tattoo while simultaneously not caring about hosting a generic design on their skin, forever.

CipherX showed me another interesting perk in development: It’s planning to sell kits that let you apply the tattoo yourself at home. At the launch event I attended, the company showed off a mockup of a palm-sized metal tin that could be shipped to a customer’s doorstep in the future. I asked CipherX for a launch timeline, but it hasn’t responded at the time of writing. CipherX is currently only offering tattoos through approved pop-ups in London, using trained practitioners to manage age verification requirements.

I don’t think Microdot patches will revolutionize tattooing, but it might make them more accessible for a wider audience of needle-fearing folk. At £50 (about $66), I feel it’s a fair price for a more comfortable tattooing experience, and comparable to the price of similarly sized tattoos, though this obviously varies by area and artist rates. My only remaining test will be to see how “permanent” these tattoos really are. While it did darken, it’s now settled to a shade of black that looks fairly washed out, as if I’d had it for years, not days.

I’ll provide an update in the coming months to show if the tattoo has changed. But for now, I have no ragrets.

Anthropic says Claude accidentally hacked real companies too

Read on theverge.com

Anthropic just realized several of its Claude AI models hacked into the systems of three different organizations during testing, acting on their own and without the company noticing. The revelation comes days after rival OpenAI said one of its own models had breached developer platform Hugging Face, adding to growing unease over whether frontier AI labs are doing enough to control the increasingly capable systems they are building. 

In a blog post describing the incidents, Anthropic said Claude gained unauthorized access to the systems during cybersecurity evaluations. All of the attacks happened during “capture-the-flag” exercises, a common way of testing hacking ability, where models are asked to find and obtain hidden information inside of a simulated network. 

The disclosure adds to mounting pressure on frontier AI labs in the wake of the Hugging Face incident and the release of powerful open-weight Chinese models. Employees at the major labs are now calling for coordinated global governance, and US lawmakers have begun weighing tighter oversight of powerful models and who can access them. 

Anthropic says the environment for its cybersecurity tests was supposed to be isolated. However, a “misconfiguration” left the machines Claude accessed “with live internet access,” the company said, and because all models had been “explicitly told” they had no internet access, they “assumed” the real networks it encountered were part of the simulated environment.

The earliest incidents date back to April and involved three different Claude models: Opus 4.7, Mythos 5, and “an internal research test model,” according to the blog post. As the models were being tested on their cyber abilities, Anthropic said they lacked the standard safeguards usually put in place to curtail riskier behavior. 

The company said it discovered incidents after reviewing more than 141,000 cybersecurity test runs, something it only did after OpenAI disclosed its rogue AI agent was behind the attack on Hugging Face.  

The three models behaved very differently when they encountered information suggesting that the systems they were encountering were, in fact, real. By Anthropic’s account, the oldest model, Opus 4.7, recognized it had reached a real system, “but continued its attack.” Its flagship Mythos 5 figured out it was using the internet but somehow reasoned this was all still part of the simulation, so continued. The internal test model, which Anthropic describes as “our latest model,” stopped the exercise when evidence emerged that its targets were real. 

Anthropic did not identify the affected organizations and said it will continue to investigate the incident and provide updates when it can. The company said it is also speaking with AI research nonprofit METR about conducting a third-party review of what happened. OpenAI has also hired METR to conduct an independent review. 

Throughout the post, Anthropic repeatedly contrasts both the nature and its handling of the incidents with OpenAI’s, ending with a bulleted, four-point list outlining the differences — and why it believes its own response was better. Anthropic emphasizes that it “proactively” reviewed its tests, and did so before a company detected any activity. It also said its models accessed the internet “via an open path,” rather than using a novel exploit like OpenAI’s agent, adding that its most recent model also stopped when it realized it was working in a real environment. 

Anthropic also said its models failed in a different way from OpenAI’s agent, indicating that this was a safer form of failure. “While there is not a perfectly sharp distinction between the two, we believe these incidents to be closer to a harness and operational failure than a model alignment failure,” the company said. In plain English: The Claude models were doing what they were told, while OpenAI’s agent pursued its goal in a way its creators did not intend, described as misalignment in the AI safety world.

Anthropic called on other AI labs to conduct similar proactive reviews of its cyber testing, adding that the discovery underscores the need for stronger controls and safety measures when testing AI systems.

New York sues Kalshi for allegedly running an ‘illegal gambling operation’

Read on theverge.com

New York is suing Kalshi over claims the prediction market is running “an illegal gambling operation.” In the lawsuit, New York Attorney General Letitia James accuses Kalshi of violating state laws by accepting wagers without a license from the state’s gaming commission, as reported earlier by CNBC.

An investigation from the Office of the Attorney General (OAG) found Kalshi exposes New York residents “to serious personal and financial risk,” including people under the legal gambling age of 21, the lawsuit alleges. The state asks the court to block Kalshi — which is headquartered in New York — from operating in the state and to pay restitution to customers who placed bets on the platform.

New York joins Nevada, New Mexico, Arizona, Washington, and others in suing Kalshi. Though some states, such as Wisconsin, Illinois, and Rhode Island, have attempted to introduce laws regulating Kalshi and other prediction markets like Polymarket, they have faced pushback from the federal government. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) says it has “exclusive jurisdiction” over prediction markets, but many states argue this authority doesn’t apply to sports wagering.

“It’s sad to see this type of political theater from the leadership in our own state,” Elisabeth Diana, Kalshi’s head of communications, said in an emailed statement to The Verge. “This would also hurt New Yorkers, who would be driven offshore.”

Beyond state gambling laws, Kalshi has also sparked concerns about insider trading. Earlier this month, ABC News reported that President Donald Trump’s teleprompter operator made $100,000 placing bets on Kalshi related to Trump’s speeches.

The major labels propose rules to keep AI slop off the charts

Read on theverge.com

Several record labels, including the big three — Universal Music Group, Sony Music, and Warner Music Group — have proposed rules regarding chart eligibility for AI songs. In short, they wouldn’t be. 

The proposal goes quite a bit further than a labeling proposal put forth by the RIAA, the International Federation of the Phonographic Industry (IFPI), SAG-AFTRA, and others. That would create a set of standardized labels for AI-generated and AI-assisted music. The labels’ proposal would require songs be clearly labeled, but it would also keep them off international charts unless they met specific criteria, including being “substantially human made.” 

To be eligible, the songs would also have to respect the terms of service of whatever AI service was used, the model would have to have the rights to any data it was trained on, and “not raise stream or chart manipulation concerns.” What sort of concerns and what constitutes “substatially human made” are currently vague. Sony Music, UMG, and Mom+Pop Music did not immediately respond to a request for clarification.

The IFPI has thrown its weight behind the labels’ proposal, though no charting organization has signaled any immediate plan to adopt the rules, the full list of which are:

Recordings developed with generative AI services must not be included in official charts where there is reason to believe that they do not satisfy all of the following criteria:

    Any generative AI services used to develop the recording are properly authorised and lawful.

    They are substantially human made.

    They do not raise stream or chart manipulation concerns.

    They comply with applicable laws, including those relating to copyright, related rights, and personality rights.

    The making available of the recordings does not breach the terms of the generative AI service used.

    The use of generative AI services to develop the recording is appropriately signalled to consumers on downstream services (e.g., digital streaming platforms), in accordance with any applicable legislation and/or industry labelling standards.

    Here’s the problem with putting an AI image generator in Google Earth

    Read on theverge.com

    A text prompt was all it took to generate reality-warping images using Google Earth’s satellite, aerial, and 3D imagery with a now-rolled back AI feature, like these images generated by Digital Digging’s Henk van Ess that show “refugees near the Mexican border” and a bomb crater near a hospital in Gaza. 

    Google initially responded to Digital Digging’s AI-altered images, saying, “We take misinformation seriously – every image created with Nano Banana in Google Earth includes the SynthID digital watermark, so if someone is unsure about an image, they can ask the Gemini app or use Lens in Search to see if the image was AI-generated.” In addition to using Google’s “@verifyai” tag in Gemini to check suspicious images, van Ess suggests comparing them to other satellite platforms, like Sentinel-2 or Landsat, and even verifying orbital data like when it says the image was taken and by which satellite.

    The feature launched globally on the web version of Google Earth earlier this week, and Google’s blog post pitched it as a tool for visualizing historical sites and real estate projects. Some images generated with it were easy to spot as fakes, like one showing the Sphinx crossed with the Statue of Liberty, but I can see how the Nano Banana 2 feature could have created images believable enough to spread misinformation. And while Google’s watermark may be difficult to remove, it’s not impossible to miss or bypass – Digital Digging was able to fool Hive’s AI detector with an AI-altered video from Google Earth.

    Update, July 31st: After publication, Google announced it’s rolling back the feature.

    The ban on robot vacuums won’t make them safer, only worse

    Read on theverge.com

    No other gadget knows as much about your home as a robot vacuum. They map your space, learn your routines, and many now carry a camera and microphone into every room in your house. As AI gives them a better understanding of what they see, the privacy and security risks these autonomous robots pose are only growing.

    That’s why greater scrutiny makes sense, but the ban on foreign-made mobile robots issued by the Federal Communications Commission earlier this week — implemented to address “unacceptable risks to the national security of the United States or the safety and security of United States persons” — doesn’t actually judge products by how secure they are, just where they’re built. It effectively means no new robot vacuums from major companies can enter the US market unless they are built here. And spoiler alert: none are.

    Cybersecurity and manufacturing are not the same thing. If the goal is safer robot vacuums (and robot lawnmowers, companion bots, humanoids, and all the other robots this ban sweeps up), this policy misses the mark. A meaningful security standard for robots would require encrypted map storage, security audits, vulnerability disclosures, and clear data retention policies.

    Instead, this ban means Americans will see fewer new models, slower innovation, and more expensive robot vacuums. In short, we’ll get worse vacuums than those we have today.

    I’ve been covering and testing robot vacuums for over a decade, and have seen dozens of privacy and security stories. The fear that your robot vacuum could spy on you or leak your data isn’t paranoia; it’s a well-documented fact that we have personal experience with here at The Verge. Many of the industry’s largest manufacturers — including Dreame, Ecovacs, Roborock, iRobot, and Shark — have suffered security vulnerabilities or data breaches.

    Those failures don’t point toward regulating manufacturing, but toward the need for stronger cybersecurity requirements, like those defined by the government’s Cyber Trust Mark program. But that program doesn’t exist yet, and if it ever does, it will be entirely voluntary.

    This manufacturing ban applies to any foreign-made robot, but footnotes in the FCC’s public notice indicate that China is a particular concern, warning that foreign governments could use vulnerabilities found in advanced robotics to access sensitive data. China’s commerce ministry has already called the policy “discriminatory and suppressive against Chinese companies and products.”

    There are real security advantages to domestic robot production. Mehul Nariyawala, president and co-founder of Matic, the only robot vacuum currently assembled in the US (although with too many foreign parts to be considered US-made), points out that domestic manufacturing makes it easier to ensure there are no hardware backdoors and to hold companies accountable for any failures.

    But a robot vacuum made in Massachusetts isn’t automatically more secure than one made in Shenzhen. And the FCC’s ban doesn’t actually take steps to ensure they will be.

    The robot ban is a carbon copy of those the FCC has issued to Wi-Fi routers and drones over the last few months. So far, it has already granted several waivers to router companies that don’t appear to be moving their manufacturing to the US, which the waivers require. This raises questions about how consistently and fairly the new rules will be enforced and whether they can actually achieve their intended effect.

    Today, the practical consequences won’t be better security — the millions of robot vacuums already in American homes remain unaffected, as do any currently sold models. If these really are a risk, and we’ve seen plenty of evidence they can be, why leave the largest source of that risk exactly where it is? 

    In an optimistic future, the ban could spur American manufacturing. Nariyawala believes it “creates the conditions for American startups to design, engineer, and build all manner of trustworthy robots at scale.” He adds that consumer robots are where robotics manufacturing reaches scale, and the supply chains they require are the same ones defense, industrial, and healthcare robots will depend on. “Get it right, educate customers to demand it, and that domestic supply chain stops being an aspiration and becomes an industry,” he says.

    But even if he’s right, and he has more incentive than most to hope he is, that transition will take years. Today’s Matic relies heavily on imported parts, and its price is going up to $1,495 in September, showing how far building these products in the US is from replacing the $300 robot vacuum you can buy at Walmart. Meanwhile, restricting the import of new products means US households will miss out on the next wave of innovations in a fast-moving category.

    Chinese manufacturers may have started out as fast followers, but they have transformed the robot vacuum industry over the past decade. Fierce competition brought better navigation, improved obstacle avoidance, self-emptying docks, and much better mopping systems. That competition also squeezed American manufacturers out of business; iRobot, the American pioneer in this space, filed for bankruptcy last year and is now owned by its Chinese manufacturing partner. 

    Without that competitive pressure, progress in the US market will slow, and prices will rise. But the competition I would most like to see isn’t who can build the wildest appendages or produce the highest suction power — it’s competing on privacy; I want a robot vacuum that doesn’t require an internet connection to clean my house, maps that stay on the device instead of the cloud; object recognition that runs locally instead of on remote servers; and clear explanations of what data leaves my house and why.

    Matic does much of this today, which is one reason it’s my top pick of the many robot vacuums I’ve tested, but it’s a high price to pay for what should be easy features to deliver.

    It feels too late to save the American robot vacuum industry. But iRobot co-founder and former CEO Colin Angle, whose startup Familiar Machines & Magic is developing a robotic companion that could fall under this ban, supports the ban’s stated goals. “We see this as part of a broader moment for US robotics, one where the opportunity lies in building domestic leadership,” he told me. “That means continued investment in domestic manufacturing, R&D, and talent, and we’re committed to being part of that effort as the category grows.”

    If that is the goal, it may well succeed, but only because it shuts out the competition — which is the same thing Angle argues the Chinese government did to help its burgeoning robot vacuum market a decade ago. But if the goal is making robots more secure, we’re still floundering.

    Meanwhile, Chinese robot vacuum makers and their US-based users are in limbo. Current products will continue to work and be sold, but the path forward is murky at best. One bright spot is that current products can continue to get security updates; the FCC doesn’t regulate software patches unless they change the radio characteristics of a device.

    Kristen Marion, global PR brand lead at Eufy, owned by Anker, told me that they plan to continue to sell, support, and update existing products, adding that “Like others across the industry, we are seeking further clarity on how the rule will be applied to future products, and we will keep our customers informed.” Narwal was more bullish. Yunlin Kong, president of Narwal Overseas Business, told me, “We’re not going anywhere. The US is one of our most important markets, and our commitment here is unchanged.” I reached out to several other Chinese manufacturers who either said much the same thing as Eufy or politely declined to comment.

    The concerns driving the ban are legitimate. Robot vacuums deserve more scrutiny than they’ve received, especially as AI expands their capabilities far beyond the original purpose of cleaning our floors. But a broad manufacturing ban that ignores what’s already in your house and can’t tell a secure product from an insecure one isn’t solving that problem. It’s just slowing down the only thing that’s actually worked in our favor: competition. If the government wants safer robot vacuums, don’t regulate where they’re made; regulate how securely they work.

    With Switch 2, iPhone, and laptop tricks, the Sharge Disk Pro 2 is finally a worthy EDC

    Read on theverge.com

    Sharge, the company that makes delightful retro Mac-shaped chargers and see-inside batteries, is finally impressing me with a portable SSD. I couldn’t recommend the Sharge Disk, Disk Plus, or even the Disk Pro, but I’d be happy to own the new Disk Pro 2 Ultra.

    This isn’t just a drive. It’s a 4K HDMI dock and a USB-C hub too. It’s a veritable USB-C Swiss Army Knife for all your main gadgets: laptop, tablet, phone, Steam Deck, even the Nintendo Switch and Switch 2.

    And this USB multitool has built-in MagSafe pattern magnets to stick it to your iPhone, Pixel, or anything where you’re willing to stick a magnetic ring. (One stick-on ring is included.)

    In total, this pack-of-playing-cards-sized gadget adds one 4K 144Hz HDMI 2.1 video output, a 5Gbps USB-A port, SD and microSD card readers, plus USB-C passthrough charging — plus a slot to add up to 8TB of solid-state storage with a full-sized M.2 2280 (or smaller M.2 2242 and 2230) stick SSDs.

    By plugging in a single cord, I gave my MacBook Pro a second external monitor, four simultaneous sources of storage (SSD, USB flash drive, SD, microSD), and 80W of USB-C PD juice.

    And when I plugged in my Android phone and a USB-C iPhone, both of them saw the SSD and showed up on my HDMI screen. It also unlocked 4K60 ProRes video recording on the iPhone… more on that in a minute.

    Some of these features exist together in other gadgets, and this 10Gbps external SSD enclosure certainly isn’t the fastest in town now that 40Gbps and 80Gbps enclosures exist. With big 100GB and 195GB transfers, my write speeds with a budget 1TB Lexar NM710 in the Disk Pro 2 averaged around 700MB/s.

    But that’s still nothing to sneeze at, and how many enclosures double as USB-C HDMI docks, let alone ones that speak Nintendo’s language and magnetically attach to your phone? How many have a built-in fan to keep them cool? And how many are low-power enough that you can power the HDMI and USB ports from your phone or laptop, no need for a power cord?

    Things get a little tricky with the low wattage, though.

    First, Sharge is selling two models of the Disk Pro 2, the “Ultra” that requires at least 1.5W of USB-C bus power, and and the “Lite” which needs 5W for its more power-hungry chips (and only does 4K60 over HDMI 2.0, instead of 4K144 over HDMI 2.1).

    Second, to achieve such low power draw on the Ultra, it can only route 3W of power from your laptop or phone to a connected SSD, Sharge marketing manager Leo H. tells me. Reviews show my borrowed Lexar NM710 draws more than that during writes — and sure enough, the Disk Pro 2 shut down right after I started ProRes video recording on an iPhone 15 Pro or a big transfer on my Galaxy S25.

    (There’s a simple solution: just plug an external USB-C battery or charger into the Disk Pro 2. When I did that, I recorded over five minutes of 4K60 ProRes video, no problem. You could instead try to find a lower-power SSD.)

    Third, while the HDMI dock does work with both my Switch 2 and my original Switch, Nintendo’s handhelds explicitly check for a relatively specific external power source before docking. Sharge is still tweaking the firmware, but says I currently need a 85W or higher USB-C PD charger plugged in for Switch 2 in particular.

    You should also know there’s a long pause before the Nintendo Switch and Switch 2 appear on a connected TV, like some other third-party docks, and you won’t get variable refresh rate (VRR) with a Steam Deck or other PC handhelds. It does 4K144 and 4K120 with HDR, but the Steam Deck claims my VRR TV is not VRR-capable with the Disk Pro 2 as a go-between.

    And while I successfully worked with a mouse, keyboard, headset, monitor, webcam, and more plugged into the Disk Pro 2 for several days — by daisy-chaining an extra USB hub — I did occasionally see the monitor go dark for a minute, roughly once a day.

    There’s still work for Sharge to do. But it’s the first drive from the company that I’d happily add to my go bag.

    The original Sharge Disk was a bulky dongle with a ridiculously noisy tiny fan. The Disk Plus marketed itself as barely-there stylishly thin SSD enclosure that magnetically attaches to your phone, but the magnets were actually a stick-on accessory that made the drive much thicker. The first Sharge Disk Pro had a power port so tiny and recessed that most charging cables wouldn’t fit, and buttons that could actually cut power to the drive mid-transfer.

    The Disk Pro 2 fixes all that. The included cable is now detachable, the magnets are built-in, the ports are no longer weirdly recessed, the fan isn’t noisy by default, you can turn off that fan entirely, the switch doesn’t cut power to the drive, and the drive has a larger airflow path. Plus, you can bring your own SSD — though I’d prefer toolless swaps to the current screws.

    You won’t always see much higher transfer speeds turning that fan on full blast. My 195GB transfer took 4 minutes and 55 seconds with the fan on full power, and 5 minutes 5 seconds with it off — a difference of just 10 seconds, even though I let it cool between test. The real difference is the one I felt when trying to pick up the Disk Pro 2 afterwards. Without fan, the metal sides were burning hot to the touch.

    The Disk Pro 2 Ultra is $75 on Kickstarter for early bird and will be $89 full retail. While the typical caveats apply with Kickstarter projects, Sharge has a very solid track record for shipping (even if it’s hit and miss on design).
