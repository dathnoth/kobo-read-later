The News

Surface Laptop Ultra is nice Windows laptop with an Nvidia CPU & GPU

Microsoft launched those fancy new computers they teased a few months ago - the Surface Laptop Ultra and Surface RTX Spark Dev Box. The laptop pairs an NVIDIA Blackwell RTX GPU, with an ARM-based NVIDIA Grace CPU and 128GB of unified memory, which when combined, they refer to as NVIDIA RTX Spark. Apparently the 15" screen has the "brightest peak HDR of any laptop", specifically calling out the MacBook Pro. It's also got Magnetic Connect, "the world's first built-in magnetic USB-C charging on a laptop". Aussie pricing starts at $4,399 and goes up to $9,649 if you want the 128GB unit. The RTX Spark Dev Box is a desktop PC similar in specs to the laptop, but with a bigger thermal envelope so it should be a bit snappier. Unfortunately, they're not selling it outside the USA (where it costs ~A$8700), which sucks as I think it's a really cool looking computer. Nicer than the Mac Studio IMHO.

Google Playground & Unity Spark "lower the barrier" to game development

Google burdened the world today with Playground, "an experimental gaming platform that lets you create, play, and share custom games". It "lowers the barriers to creation, so bringing game ideas to life can now be as easy as describing it through a few prompts". If anything worth playing that isn't a themed derivative of an existing game is ever concocted with Playground, please let me know. Unity, the mob behind the engine used in so many games these days, also released a similar, perhaps slightly more advanced AI game builder today called Spark. Aftermath said about Spark: "everything made here looks like ass, even though this was their carefully-curated corporate trailer" and "Unity is supposed to be a tool for serious game development; this is an insult to everyone who has ever tried to actually learn how to make a video game".

Firmus IPO has an "unprecedented lack of detail" and struggling

What was lined up to be the biggest IPO in ASX history, potentially making "AI factory" builder Firmus one of Australia's top 10 companies, fizzled out and may even close the IPO early at a drastically lower valuation. According to the ABC, participants in the IPO are "underwhelmed" with the roadshow which had an "unprecedented lack of detail" and investors put off by the huge amounts of debt Firmus has on its books. It's still scheduled to float on the ASX October 23rd, but at around $6/share instead of the expected $11/share. Oh, and it came out on Tuesday, one day in to the Firmus IPO roadshow, that "Firmus and CDC mutually agreed earlier this year not to proceed with the previously proposed Southgate development partnership" - not a good thing to have kept quiet going in to an IPO. I think the only thing they have going for them is a line of credit on some Nvidia GPUs direct from Jensen - everything else about Firmus smells off.

RIP Margaret Hamilton, the first software engineer, programming legend

Margaret Hamilton died on September 30th. If that name means nothing to you, she was the original software engineer and best known for her work as the first programmer on NASA's Apollo space missions and lead development on the on-board flight software for the crewed missions. Of the experience, she said "because software was a mystery, a black box, upper management gave us total freedom and trust. We had to find a way, and we did. Looking back, we were the luckiest people in the world; there was no choice but to be pioneers". MIT, where she worked for almost 20 years in the 50s, 60s and 70s has a great obituary, as does the New York Times. An absolute giant in the field of software and paved the way for much of what we take for granted now in computing.

Image Of The Day

With the anniversary of that moon landing approaching, Google set out to shine a light on Margaret's influence on Apollo, and on the field of software engineering itself. The tribute was created by positioning over 107,000 mirrors at the Ivanpah Solar Facility in the Mojave Desert to reflect the light of the moon, instead of the sun, like the mirrors normally do. The result is a 1.4-square-mile portrait of Margaret, bigger than New York's Central Park. (Google)
