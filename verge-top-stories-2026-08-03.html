China’s Alibaba takes another swipe at America’s AI supremacy

Read on theverge.com

Chinese tech giant Alibaba released what it says is its largest and “most capable AI model to date,” claiming performance rivaling the best systems from US frontier labs Anthropic and OpenAI, as well as domestic rivals like Moonshot AI’s Kimi K3.  Alibaba said it was making the model, Qwen3.8-Max, widely available to users in a blog post published on Monday. The release had been expected after the company previewed the model last month, when it claimed it was “second only to Fable 5,” Anthropic’s flagship.

The open release of another highly capable Chinese AI model adds to sky-high tensions along multiple fronts in Silicon Valley and Washington over how to safely manage AI systems and retain the US’ technological edge over China. 

Results from Alibaba’s own testing shared on Monday suggests it’s as powerful as claimed, as does its ranking on crowdsourced model-comparison platform Arena.AI. Alibaba’s own testing shows the model’s performance to broadly match — and sometimes exceed — that of Fable 5 on benchmark tests. On the Arena text model leaderboard, Qwen3.8-Max trails only Fable 5 and three models in Anthropic’s Opus family. For frontend coding, it is beaten only by two Claude Opus models and Kimi K3, and for visual analysis, only Fable 5 trounces it.   

Alibaba says Qwen3.8-Max has 2.4 trillion parameters, a numerical measure of the settings a model learns during training that it uses to process data, recognize patterns, and undertake various tasks. While parameter counts are widely used as a shorthand for model performance, a bigger number does not always mean better. Moonshot’s Kimi K3 model has 2.8 trillion parameters, but most top American labs keep figures private, and neither OpenAI nor Anthropic disclose exact counts for their top systems.

The Chinese company said it will release the weights for Qwen3.8-Max next week. Weights are the adjustable numerical values of an AI that determine how it processes information. Open-weight systems, while more restrictive than traditional open-source software, give developers far more control than they have over proprietary products from companies like OpenAI and Anthropic. Qwen3.8-Max marks a return to open-weight releases for Alibaba after the company briefly pivoted towards proprietary releases for its more advanced models earlier this year. 

Open-weight releases have become a growing point of differentiation for China’s AI industry, where they have become the norm. Moonshot released Kimi K3’s last week and many other top AI models are also open-weight. Beijing has championed the strategy as a means of growing China’s influence in global AI governance and encouraging widespread adoption of domestic tech champions.  

Alibaba’s release intensifies competition with China, whose firms appear to be rapidly narrowing the gap with US companies and have increased the tempo of releases in recent weeks. Qwen3.8-Max closely follows the release of Kimi K3, viewed as another challenge to American AI dominance, and both ByteDance and MiniMax released capable new video generation models on Friday.   

Openness has also proved to be a divisive issue. Amid reports of potential crackdown on open tools in the wake of the Chinese releases, the US industry has largely rallied around preserving access to open-weight models, both as a safety necessity and as means of preserving competition. That debate comes as closed-model providers, notably OpenAI and Anthropic, face increasing scrutiny after revealing a slew of cyberattacks unknowingly perpetrated by their own escaped AI agents. Incident reports from one victim suggest the restrictive safety rails intended to stop nefarious use of AI models also limits their use as defensive tools.

Big Walk is like co-op Breath of the Wild

Read on theverge.com

Untitled Goose Game is a tough act to follow. It was a silly experience that captured what I imagine it would feel like to be a sentient goose: a lot of waddling, a lot of honking, and a lot of shenanigans. That’s why Big Walk, the next game from Goose Game developer House House, feels like a radical and unexpected departure. Instead of playing as a goose terrorizing a small village, Big Walk is a co-op multiplayer game where you explore a large island and have to work together to solve puzzles. I’ve been playing Big Walk as a duo with my wife, and while we’re not done yet, we’ve spent more than 13 hours exploring the island and solving problems together. Big Walk may be a comparatively small game, but the experience of making unexpected discoveries can make it feel like a grand adventure in the Zelda mold. 

The name Big Walk suggests a game about walking — and there is indeed much walking — but it’s really more about talking. Outside of some brief signage about the controls, the game doesn’t give you explicit guidance about how to play, where to go, or what puzzles to tackle. Instead, you have to rely on communicating with your fellow players over proximity chat (or a neat proximity text system) to figure things out. Puzzles can scale to fit two to four players, but as many as 12 people can inhabit a game at the same time.

You play as sentient spheres with arms and legs that don’t look like any species I’ve ever seen, tasked with collecting curious red balls we’ve been calling “nubbins.” You’ll need to stay in touch to navigate the island, which is filled with bushes, sloping hills, and odd buildings that make it easy to get lost. When the sun sets, things get really dark, which forces you to use lanterns and stick together. Big Walk also doesn’t give you a map menu or golden arrows telling you where to go, so it’s up to you and your friends to figure out how to traverse the island.

Big Walk is crafted so that something interesting is usually hidden just around the corner, like a new landmark, a tool of some kind, and even tall towers. More often than not, you’ll stumble across some kind of brightly colored building that features a puzzle you have to work together to solve, like chatting from opposite sides of a wall to figure out how to put some tiles in the correct order. Sometimes, Big Walk even separates players so that you can’t use proximity chat at all.

Coming up with ways to communicate is difficult given that your character can basically only point, jump, and hold things. That means the moments when my wife and I have been separated to solve a puzzle have genuinely been nail-biters. My heart was actually racing when I kicked a ticking kitchen timer up a hill and hoped that I had given my wife enough time to drop it in the right spot. Often, when you have to figure out some sort of code to solve a puzzle, the game forces you to wait for a few agonizing seconds to know if you completed it correctly. When you do get one done, you’ll see a joyful burst of confetti and get a new nubbin.

The way Big Walk is structured has some pitfalls. You’ll spend a lot of time just hiking around the island to get somewhere new, which can be tedious. You can’t do much in the game without at least one other person, so that means you have to match up when you want to play with whoever else is in your group. At the end of a long day, sometimes my wife and I didn’t want to hop into the game to walk slowly, get lost, and solve hard puzzles. Our conversations were often punctuated by yawns, which any fellow parents with a toddler will understand.

But Big Walk’s constraints are also what make it so remarkable. While hiking around and promising ourselves we’d seek out a single puzzle for the evening, we’d often decompress about our day and catch up on things. When we found a puzzle, we’d get extremely invested in figuring it out. After we solved it, we’d usually have the energy to tackle another one. We even figured out how to use the limited moveset to give each other high-fives and hugs.

Many of the elements that made Goose Game great are present in Big Walk. There’s a lot of walking, a lot of talking, and a lot of shenanigans. While playing Big Walk, however, I also kept thinking of a very different game. The sense of discovery and joy are much like what made The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild so special, whether that was finding a kooky way to solve a puzzle, stumbling upon an extremely useful item, or climbing to the highest peak and taking in the view. But unlike Breath of the Wild, Big Walk let me share the world, and those moments of discovery, with my best friend.

Big Walk launches August 4th on the Nintendo Switch 2, PS5, and Steam.

Lenovo is expected to release some of the first Googlebook models later this year, and leaked images have now given us a good idea of what they might look like. The 9100 Pro is Samsung’s first SSD to support PCIe Gen 5, making it a great fit if you have the right motherboard and other parts to get as much speed from it as possible. Its theoretical maximum random read and write speeds are at least twice as fast as the 990 Pro SSD that it replaced. However, it’s good (albeit slower than advertised) in a PCIe Gen 4 setup, as well.

It’ll work fine without a heatsink in a PC or laptop (though, it’s never a bad idea to use one that fits in your system). However, you’ll need to install a heatsink in order to use this in a PlayStation 5, due to potential heat issues that could damage the SSD if used without one. Thankfully, heatsinks are super affordable, and come in a range of designs and price points.

Spider-Man and The Odyssey are splitting up IMAX screens after a record-breaking weekend

Read on theverge.com

Spider-Man: Brand New Day is joining The Odyssey in IMAX theaters after both movies led the biggest weekend in box office history. On Monday, IMAX announced that Spider-Man: Brand New Day will be available in select digital IMAX locations across North America starting Thursday, August 6th.

The news comes as The Odyssey’s exclusive three-weekend run in IMAX theaters comes to an end, allowing Spider-Man: Brand New Day to play alongside it, as reported by Variety. Spider-Man: Brand New Day raked in $355 million following its July 31st release, making it the second-biggest box office debut in North America since Avengers: Endgame in 2019. Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey had a similarly big weekend, pulling in $51 million weeks after its premiere. Both films — in addition to Toy Story 5, Moana, Minions & Monsters — led to a record-breaking $430 million weekend at the box office, according to Variety.

The Odyssey will continue playing in theaters across the global IMAX network. The film has already generated $221 million at the global IMAX box office, helping to make July IMAX’s highest-grossing month in history. IMAX says The Odyssey is the only film that has driven “multiple $40 million-plus weekends” in its global box office.

Bluesky’s new CEO wants a big tent, not a bubble

Read on theverge.com

Today, I’m talking with Toni Schneider, who is the brand new CEO of the social platform Bluesky — he formally took over after a short stint as interim CEO. This is one of my favorite kinds of interviews to do on Decoder, because a couple years ago, we had Bluesky’s prior CEO, Jay Graber, on the show — she’s now the company’s chief innovation officer, and all of that kind of shuffle is pure Decoder bait. And a new CEO often brings with them new priorities, which is perhaps the most Decoder subject of all.

Bluesky in particular is a company pulling in a lot of different directions: There’s a Twitter-like social media app, which is what most people are familiar with, and then there’s the underlying technology behind that app call AT Protocol, which allows anyone to build social networking products that interoperate with Bluesky. As you’ll hear Toni say, that ecosystem is vibrant and growing — it’s called the ATmosphere, which is adorable.

But the users of Bluesky the app are not the same as the people building on AT Protocol, and the goals of a company founded to push forward the ideas of open and interoperable social standards are not necessarily aligned with the things all those users might need or want. This is a fight that bursts into view on Bluesky itself all the time, and I wanted to know how Toni thinks about balancing all of that with what remains a fairly small team.

Toni was formerly the CEO of Automattic, which makes the huge WordPress web content management platform, and you’ll hear him make the case that this period in social networking feels a lot like the early days of the web, and that success looks like a vibrant ecosystem with lots of big players that might take a decade to fully develop.

That’s quite a vision, but here and now, the open web is falling apart, and the social products of today are fragmenting and in many cases either get smaller or full of AI slop. So we talked about all of that, and about Bluesky’s reputation as a bastion of leftists insulated in their own little bubble sitting next to the increasingly hard right bubble of X. And of course, we talked about how Bluesky might start to generate revenue — and interestingly enough, it’s not just ads.

This is a fun one that touches on almost every Decoder theme there is. I think you’re gonna like it.

Okay: Bluesky CEO Toni Schneider. Here we go.

This interview has been lightly edited for length and clarity.

Toni Schneider, you are a venture partner at True Ventures, and more importantly, the now permanent CEO of BlueSky. Welcome to Decoder.

Thank you. Thanks for having me.

I am very excited to talk to you. What is happening with the open social web is maybe the most important thing happening for media. It does not get as much attention as AI, but there’s something very big happening on these interoperable social networks. I know that you are a believer in it in the same way that you believed in the web when you were the CEO of Automattic, which is the company that makes WordPress. So let’s just start at the very start. Describe what you think BlueSky is today and what you think the opportunities with these new kinds of networks are.

BlueSky today is really two things. There’s the BlueSky app, which is what a lot of people are familiar with. It’s a social network. We think it’s a better social network. It’s more human focused, doesn’t have ads. It doesn’t have algorithms. But more importantly, underneath BlueSky is an open protocol called AT Protocol. And BlueSky is only one of now over a thousand apps built on that protocol.

The whole point of BlueSky was to demonstrate that this open protocol could scale and attract other builders, building lots of apps, lots of social experiences that all interoperate and connect with each other, which is what we believe the future of social media should look like. It’s what is actually going to make social media something that we believe in, which is that the future of social media should serve the public good. That is what motivates us, and that’s why we built the protocol first and then the app.

There’s a lot in there already that is just pure Decoder bait, including the notion that BlueSky, the product, and the ATmosphere and AT Protocol, the standard, are all intermixed in one company that you now run. Right next to that is maybe the purest Decoder bait of all: the fact that Jay Graber, who was the CEO of BlueSky – she was on the show about two years ago – stepped down into the role of chief innovation officer. You were the interim CEO, and now after four months, you are the full-time CEO. Fundamentally, that is just the story that Decoder loves to tell the most. Talk me through that. What made you step in as the interim CEO, and why did you decide to become the full-time CEO?

It’s actually really cool and inspiring that a founder, after she worked on this for six years, got it off the ground. She started inside Twitter, got it out of there, made it an independent thing. There are a lot of reasons why BlueSky is facing long odds and she got into this place, and then felt like it’s at a scale now where [it’s in] her sweet spot, the things that she loves working on, which is vision, innovation, new ideas. She felt pulled back towards building, especially also because of the advent of AI and how AI and AT Proto can interoperate. She felt like that’s what she wants to go work on, and it’s time to find somebody else for the CEO job.

I was already involved with this project because I met the team a little bit over two years ago, probably around the same time Jay was on your podcast. We became investors through True Ventures. So I’ve been following it as an investor, supporting it. The team approached me and said that my background with WordPress and open source was a really good fit. And so I agreed to be interim CEO initially because I was still a general partner at True and it’s a firm I helped build. It’s an amazing firm that I didn’t want to leave behind. But also, at True, we’ve had this model where we’ve moved back and forth between operator and investor. I did that while I was running Automattic.

So I stepped into it. After working on it hands-on for a few months, it just got more and more fun, more and more energizing. You just feel the momentum. Also, True just switched to a new fund. So actually the timing was good where I could say, “Okay, I will switch my true role to part-time, become a venture partner,” which is what I used to be when I ran Automattic, “and I’ll go all in at BlueSky.”

Tell me about the difference between being the interim CEO and the full-time CEO. When you’re the interim CEO, it feels like there are probably some boundaries in what you can do. And then the full-time CEO can obviously do everything that a full-time CEO can do. What did you feel like the boundaries were as interim CEO?

On the inside, it hasn’t felt very different. I actually think the move to drop the “interim” is more important on the outside, especially for the ATmosphere and the AT Proto community, and people building on this platform and in this ecosystem just to say, “No, I’m here for the duration. This isn’t just some interim gig and then somebody else is going to show up.” So I felt like, to the outside, that makes a big difference. On the inside, the job really hasn’t changed much..

So many of my conversations here in Decoder are really about the timescale of decisions. I’ll make the comparison to my newsroom because it’s easy and it’s easy to understand. Our reporters make decisions in like 30-minute increments. A story comes up, they write it, they publish it, they move on to the next thing. I make decisions maybe in like week- or month-long increments. What are we going to cover? Where are the resources going to go?

It seems like in the interim CEO role, the timescale of those decisions is necessarily short or it has the appearance of being short. And then I’m reading your note that you published when you became the full-time CEO. In the very beginning of it, you say we’re at the very beginning of the story with a decade of exciting work ahead. And I’m wondering if the timescale of the decisions you’re making now are a decade.

Yeah. It touches on something really fascinating with this project. In general with decision making, one of the frameworks I really like is the reversible and irreversible decisions. A lot of decisions we make are actually reversible. I like to optimize to just make those decisions quickly and then make sure you can undo the decisions if you need to change them.

And then there are a few decisions that really do have very long-term implications. They’re much harder to reverse. With this project in particular, there are decisions made at this protocol level that we’re going to have to live with for a long time. There’s a part of the company in this community that thinks very, very, very long term. This is a protocol that is going to be around for decades already. It’s in enough use that it’s going to be around for a long time. Those decisions you really need to be careful about. Then it goes all the way to things we try in the product that work or don’t work.

I wasn’t in the interim mindset as much because I am also an investor in the company, so I care very much about the long-term picture. What attracts me to this whole endeavor is that it can have a really important long-term impact. I always think about, okay, how will this play out? Does this truly serve the core mission? It’s okay to make some of these harder decisions now that won’t pay off for a long time because this is going to take a while.

It’s not just one company. It’s already thousands of others that are building together. That’s just a different timeframe and decision-making process than your typical just “go fast, scale as quickly as possible” [methodology]. I like it. I’m comfortable with it.

I just want to stick with BlueSky, the company that is both responsible for the standard and the protocol, and the product that a lot of end users love and sometimes hate. That is the core tension of the company. The user base of BlueSky is or is not interested in the development of AT Protocol, is or is not interested in the wider ATmosphere.

Under Jay’s tenure, the company was very focused on building out the protocol because the product literally could not exist unless the protocol was robust enough to stand up the product. You could see that back and forth. I have always felt that the company is far more interested in the protocol than the product, that the product was merely there to show what people could do.

But now the product is big. It might be waxing or waning. It will come to whether you need to turn it into TikTok or not. Tell me where you, as a CEO with a decade-long vision for all of this, see the balance inside of BlueSky between the product, the thing that looks like a Twitter app, and the protocol, the thing that should enable thousands upon thousands of other social experiences?

I see the BlueSky app and the product as enabling the ATmosphere. It’s an on-ramp, or maybe a lobby to the ATmosphere where it’s just a great way to get lots of people in, get people exposed to the idea, and then realize as you use the product, “Oh, there are other things here. This isn’t just a microblogging app.”

An example from the last couple of months that’s gone super well is this format called Standard Site that emerged. The history of that is that BlueSky’s a microblogging app. People are asking for longer-form posting. A bunch of other apps started popping up that were for newsletters and blogging. In particular, three apps called Leaflet, Pocket, and Off Print. They got together and said, “Hey, why don’t we define a format for all of us to work together on the ATmosphere for all of the posts that we publish?” We looked at it and said, “Great, we can now take that and surface it in our app.”

So now, if you’re posting a blog post on Leaflet, it just shows up in the BlueSky app and we can make that a seamless experience. This went over really well to the point where now WordPress is supporting Standard Site and lots of other publishing platforms. They’re realizing the benefit of, “If I just publish to this new format now, it’s going to show up and work across all these other platforms without me having to do anything.” It’s caused this really nice ripple effect throughout the ecosystem where people can work together just by using this format. Nobody has to do deals with each other. Nobody has to get permissions and no money has to change hands. And it’s just taken off.

I’m hoping more and more BlueSky users will see those kinds of integrations and go, “Oh, cool. I’m not just getting BlueSky content here. I’m getting content from all these other apps, all these other networks. And it just works. I don’t have to do anything. I don’t have to go sign up for 10 different apps. It’s just one signup, one account, and it just works.”

You’re describing two different kinds of things. There’s a lot of work to be done at the protocol level. There’s a lot of work to be done to support new kinds of experiences like Standard Site. Then there’s BlueSky, a product which has 45 million people, and probably needs to be substantially bigger if it wants to be the aggregator for a massive network that can displace some of the other social products out there. That needs to be a lot of things to a lot of people. It needs to find different kinds of product market fit. How big is BlueSky the company now, and how is it structured to point in two different directions?

We’re now at 50 people, still very small. The biggest challenge is clearly trying to do everything you’ve described with a small team. We do have an ecosystem of hundreds and thousands of others working on it, so that helps. We do our best to coordinate everyone.

I would say we’re making good progress. We’re seeing good momentum, especially on the ATmosphere side. The things we look at are the number of daily active apps on the network, which has doubled since the beginning of the year. It’s about to hit a thousand. We look at SDK downloads for our tools that have tripled. It’s going to hit a million downloads a month by later this year. Those are the numbers that show that not only is it scaling, but we’re able to scale it with the team that we have.

How is that team structured?

About 50 people, all remote, structured into small teams. I’m a big believer that six to eight person teams, highly aligned, highly incentivized, and motivated are the way to get things done. Probably the only way to get things done. There are three core product teams. There’s the app team that deals with the BlueSky app. There’s the ATmosphere team that does all the developer-facing stuff. And then there’s a third product team called the exploration team, and that’s a new team that Jay has stood up and is running. We actually just launched a new version of the product they’re working on, Attie. Think of it as AI layered on top of the ATmosphere and what you can do with that.

Those are our three core product teams, and they’re each customer-facing. Consumer-facing, developer-facing, and then we’ll find out from the AI product what the market fit is there. We have a bunch of support teams that support those three core teams and that are pretty typical. We’ve got a legal team, et cetera, et cetera. I would say it’s a team that’s a very low ego, high agency. That is kind of the ethos. Low drama, just a lot of get-shit-done energy. Really collaborative.

Whenever I think about new social products and how they might scale, especially with explosive user growth – up until recently, the conventional wisdom is you’ve got to scale trust and safety and moderation linearly with the size of your user base. Maybe we’re going to add AI to this and that linear relationship will break, or maybe it’ll actually get even more costly because token spend will get more costly than having human moderators, or there’ll be more false positives and we’re going to be even angrier. So you have to have more humans in the loop.

It’s really unclear to me how that’s going to work. You’ve obviously been scaling BlueSky as a consumer social product. Then you have the complexity of, “We’re hooking onto lots and lots of other products that are putting content in front of people.” How does the moderation layer work? And have you seen it be a linear increase in cost with the size of the network and the user base?

We put effort into making it easier. When BlackSky started two or three years ago, it was a big lift. You had to be very technical to do it. Now it’s gotten easier and easier. 

Finally, we haven’t talked about AI yet. AI makes it even easier. These are two different standards that do slightly different things. And some use cases for ActivityPub are great and some are great for AT Proto. 

What are use cases that are good in ActivityPub and what are use cases that are better in AT Protocol?

ActivityPub is based around this notion of a server. At the heart of it, you set up your own server, you control it, you run it, that’s your community, and then all the servers can federate if you want. You don’t have to, you can just run it by yourself, or you can plug into the federation, into the Fediverse. That’s how their system works. It works really well. They have a lot of successful governments who want to set up their own server infrastructure and run their own community infrastructure.

AT Proto has a different goal. We don’t have a server at the heart of it. We have the PDS, the personal data server that is your data. The heart of AT Proto is personal identity and portability and ownership of that so that whatever you create and the social graph that you create is owned by you and lifts wherever you want to host it. It’s really a federation of millions and millions of these tiny little personal servers that can all talk to each other. That works if your social product is focused on people, not on an entire server with a community on it.

So a slightly different shape. They interoperate too. There’s something called Bridgy Fed that connects these two worlds together. So if you’ve built products on this yourself, if you want to post or publish across all of it, you can and the end user doesn’t even have to know. That’s the beauty of an open protocol: you shouldn’t have to. Consumers of this should never even hear about these new protocols. Because it’s one of these things where you have to get the protocol right and it has to be open, but then people should just forget about it. It should just disappear.

The examples we use a lot are email and websites. If I’m on Outlook and you’re on Gmail, we can just email each other. You never think about the fact that those are two completely separate systems. It just works. But on social media, it just doesn’t work that way. If you’re on Instagram and I’m on TikTok, we can’t talk to each other. You have to do everything five different times and on five different apps, and it’s crazy making. If we get this right at the protocol level, that just goes away. All this stuff just works just like email does or just like websites do.

Our job is to make all of that work under the hood, but nobody has to think about it. In the end, you just have one account, you log in anywhere and it just works, and all your friends are there and all your data’s there. And you never have to be locked in again into somebody’s system [where] you can’t leave.

This is very nice, but it’s rare that two standards that nominally seek to achieve the same thing coexist forever. There are not two Bluetooths, there are not two Wi-Fis. There are not even two webs. People can argue with me about how many webs there are, but there are not even two webs. Do you expect ActivityPub and AT Protocol to coexist? Do you expect, I don’t know, BlueSky to be able to show content from Mastodon servers and then slowly eat ActivityPub as a protocol? That’s one very common pattern that happens in these situations. I expect long-term coexistence because both of these protocols have enough users and use cases that want to continue on. They can talk to each other when you want, but that’s not the main use of it. There are a lot of people who run ActivityPub instances who don’t even want to talk to other Mastodon servers. They just want to run their own thing. They don’t want to talk to AT Proto, but they can if they want.

I don’t know what a good example is from other platforms. But they’re both going to be around for a long time and they’re just slightly different tools. They’re both in the social space, but they’re different tools that you reach to for different types of use cases. I’ll put it this way: In my mind, both can be wildly successful, and it’s not one or the other. AT Proto doesn’t need to eat ActivityPub to be successful, or vice versa. We’ve already seen that.
