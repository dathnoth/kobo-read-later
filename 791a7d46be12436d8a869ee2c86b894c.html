The News

Australian IoT cyber security label pilot started last week

I missed this last week, but Cyber Security Minister Tony Burke launched a pilot for the Australian Cyber Security Certified labeling scheme. I can't find a public press release about it, and the news articles I did find are light on details, but it's a voluntary program that "will badge smart home Internet of Things (IoT) products with an information sticker and security rating out of 4". Kinda like those energy ratings on appliances, but for cyber security. It will apply to "smart TVs, doorbells and surveillance cameras, baby monitors, robot vacuum cleaners and solar inverters". NetComm, Telstra, ASSA ABLOY / Lockwood and Electrolux are the first group of companies to have their products tested for the scheme. The details of what a product needs to do to achieve a certain rating is briefly outlined in the linked article (would take up too much space to list here), so read that if you want more info.

Microsoft latest Patch Tuesday fixes almost 1,000 vulnerabilities

If you're a Microsoft admin that's noticed how massive Patch Tuesday has become lately, you aren't alone. Normally Microsoft's big monthly security updates fix around 100 vulnerabilities, but in June it was 200. In July it was 570. August was "only" 400, but September's (released yesterday) has patches for a whopping 966 vulnerabilities across a wide range of Microsoft products. This rapid escalation in fixes for security bugs is due to Microsoft using LLMs to try find the holes and try patch them before someone else with access to an LLM find it and exploits it. I imagine there's thousands of people (maybe even millions) pointing LLMs at Microsoft's apps, churning through a huge amount of tokens trying to see what they can crack into, so they can feast on the goo inside.

OpenAI claims to have solved Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem

I've never heard of the "infamous Navier-Stokes puzzle", but it's a big deal in math circles and was one of the Millennium Prize Problems that if you solved, would score you US$1m. Levent Alpöge, an Anthropic employee, and Tristan Buckmaster, a math professor at NYU have been working on it using AI tools and released a paper outlining "groundbreaking results that edge us towards that larger solution". OpenAI heard about these "rumors that two Millennium Prize problems had been resolved" and "used an internal model that is significantly more capable than GPT‑6 Astra" to achieve what they claim is an answer to the Millennium Prize Problem for Navier-Stokes. The situation between OpenAI and Buckmaster and Alpöge is messy, but putting all that aside (OpenAI being jerks is par for the course), it appears that LLMs are becoming pretty useful tools for many types of science. I don't know if it's all hype and hot air, but something's cooking here, yeah?

Something I Saw On The Internet

Phrack and tmpout are cool internet hacker magazines for nerds nerdier than me

A few weeks ago I mentioned Paged Out in The Sizzle and youse liked that, so I think you'd like Phrack - a 40 year old hacker "magazine" that's published all in plaintext ASCII. There hasn't been an issue since August 2025 and most of the topics are way over my head, but damn it's cool. I should put more ASCII art in The Sizzle, but if I publish it in plaintext there's no links and I'm a big fan of hyperlinks. If you like this kinda thing, you'd also like tmpout. Very similar vibe to Paged Out and Phrack, also all in ASCII. All the topics are totally over my head here too, but that's what I like about it. I'm glad stuff like this still exists on the internet.
