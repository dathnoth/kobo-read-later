The News

Microsoft & OpenAI admit that LLMs are destroying any incentive to publish news on the open web

Thanks to court documents made public as part of the New York Time's case against Microsoft and OpenAI, we have on the record proof of a Microsoft executive calling LLMs "an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions" and the "largest theft of labor in human history". Another internal Microsoft document claimed "almost no one intended for content they created to be used in this fashion, nor are they compensated for its use". A policy document from Microsoft states generative AI could "significantly disrupt the employment of the very people who generated the data on which the foundation model was trained". OpenAI called itself an "existential threat" to news publishers and an OpenAI engineer testified that "no matter how prominently we show the links, users won't click". Narcissistic sociopaths, all of them.

Toyota going all-in with 400,000 robots to assist their human production line workers

Toyota is planning to deploy 400,000 "in-house-developed humanoid robots at production sites worldwide", starting in 2028. It will introduce them gradually as they upgrade factories around the world. The robot is called ELEY (Embodied Learning robot for Enhanced Yield) and can "learn fine motor skills, such as moving parts from one box to another and folding cloth. It learns on its own and finds the optimal approach to performing tasks". Toyota's already deployed a few on production lines, with workers wearing "jigs modeled after ELEY's fingers and repeat their normal tasks, facilitating the learning". Apparently it can fold t-shirts perfectly after two weeks of training. A Toyota exec said "we aim for a world where robots coexist with humans, rather than replacing them".

Australian public service and Optus join many others in considering a ban of smart glasses in their vicinity

The minister for public service has asked the APS nerds for advice "whether smart glasses capable of recording should be prohibited in government workplaces", because the pervert goggles "raise legitimate privacy and security concerns, particularly because of their ability to record and capture information, and we need clear and consistent guidance for public servants". Meanwhile, an Optus cybersecurity executive told a conference that glasses with high quality, hidden cameras are "privacy privacy challenge" and they're considering the impacts on their use in Optus stores and offices, but isn't keen on a ban, instead wants disclosure, saying that "when you walk into a room, you say, I've got smart glasses on", similar to disclosing the use of AI, which as we know, is a thing everyone using AI does regularly and often.

Something I Saw On The Internet

Now that everyone can code, it's become clearer why many shouldn't

I've followed Paul Ford's writing for years, and last week he had a piece in the NYT exploring why AI code hasn't developed a "killer app", or a really popular, successful, piece of software yet. Read the whole thing, but here's an excerpt I liked: "For a while, I must admit, it looked as if software developer roles like mine were done for. How could we fight against tireless robots? But our industry is slowly realizing that making truly cutting-edge software still requires humans to think and work together, to maximize their skill sets and to practice their respective crafts. A.I. can write very good software, but it also makes it easy to do someone else's job badly, which is part of why all those projects fail. Now that everyone can code, it's become clearer why many shouldn't".
