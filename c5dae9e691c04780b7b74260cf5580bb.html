The Digital Duty of Care passes Parliament, comes into effect a year from now

Australia now has a Digital Duty of Care law. It passed both houses of Parliament yesterday and introduces a huge range of powers to try and get social media companies to start giving a shit about how their products impact the well-being of Australians. This law supersedes the industry codes we saw with the Online Safety Act (which, lets be honest, were bullshit and never enforced) and comes into effect a year from now.

The key term in the law is that platforms now have to protect Australians from "seriously harmful material and conduct", which includes "material promoting suicide or self-harm, child sexual abuse and exploitation, violent extremism, gender-based violence and non-consensual intimate imagery". For people under the age of 18 that harm extends to "any content promoting eating disorders or self-harm, glorifying or promoting illicit behaviour, causing serious psychological distress and promoting hostile attitudes towards women". The platforms will also have to "explicitly consider the potential addictiveness of their features – such as infinite scrolling – when assessing the risks posed by their services".

There's also the "My Feed, My Way" initiative so social media users can "opt out of personalised algorithmic recommendations in their default social media feeds", a "private right of action", so Australians can sue if they feel a tech company has been derelict in their duty of care and it has caused them "loss or damage", and "powers to establish an external dispute resolution scheme for complaints against digital platforms" - i.e: a digital platforms ombudsman so you can complain to them if a tech company does something wrong.

You can check out the full legislation - Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026 - on the Parliament of Australia website.

Govt's big AI inquiry produces report to guide future legislation of industry

A report titled "Artificial Intelligence – the Australian way", was released by the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence on Tuesday and contains 46 recommendations that are the culmination of the government's inquiry into AI that'll be used to cook up legislation to regulate the industry.

Most of the controversial recommendations are around copyright. There's a few recommendations that the government setup a system where AI companies pay rights holders for the use of their work in training models, as well as updating copyright laws to protect "Australian creatives against the specific use of generative AI to replicate or re-produce a 'likeness' of their creative work, through inference input or instruction to achieve a commercial gain" - but in the same section, recommends that the law be changed so that "frontier AI companies have the certainty necessary to train their AI models in Australia in a way that reflects Australian values and culture and that give Australian AI businesses and research the certainty they need for their own training of models and use of AI inference". Seems a bit hypocritical to me.

It's not unusual for governments to ignore any recommendations they don't like in these sorts of reports, so let's see what actually happens when it comes to legislation. I'm disappointed that there's no mandate for 100% renewable energy sources for data centres, nor is there any sort of fund or program for workers who find themselves without an income when AI takes their job. The only mention of workers when it comes to AI is that they'll "continue to examine the implications".

SpaceX gets approval for 1 million satellite datacentre constellation

The USA's Federal Communications Commission gave SpaceX permission to deploy a constellation of up to 1 million data centre satellites. SpaceX is calling the constellation Starmind and in that constellation will be hundreds of thousands of "AI1" satellites built at the Gigasat Factory, that'll be about 30m high by 75m wide, each with around 175kW of computing power on board (GPUs built at Musk's Terafab, of course), a 160 sqm liquid radiator to dissipate heat from the servers and some massive solar panels strapped to the sides to power it.

Why put a datacentre in space? SpaceX justified it to the FCC: "by directly harnessing near-constant solar power with little operating or maintenance costs, these satellites will achieve transformative cost and energy efficiency while significantly reducing the environmental impact associated with terrestrial data centers". I thought it was funny how the mobs that loaned SpaceX money are afraid the AI chips the loans are secured against may end up going to space, where they'd be impossible to repossess should SpaceX/Musk default on the loan.

The European Southern Observatory said back in July that "no more than 100,000 faint satellites, below naked eye visibility, should orbit Earth, to safeguard our ability to observe the night sky with modern telescopes" and that "light pollution from very bright satellite constellations can impact the health and functioning of life on Earth, by disrupting biological clocks and ecosystems. Large constellations also have direct impacts on air quality from the numerous launches required to send and maintain thousands of satellites, as well as from the atmospheric pollution caused as they burn up on re-entry at the end of life".

1Password got hacked, source code copied and access to CI/CD system compromised

It finally happened - on November 19th, 1Password got popped. Looks like a 1Password employee got social engineered or phished and their details were used by a hacker to "access source code and push malicious code to backend services, which downloaded administrative data pertaining to our customers", as well as "copied source code from private repositories and gained access to our CI/CD system". 1Password says multiple times in the blog post announcing the hack that "at no time were customer vault contents accessed by, or available to, the attacker".

Data the hacker got their hands on includes: Names and email addresses, account and user identifiers, account creation, last sign-in times. Public keys (which are public by design, this has no impact to user security). Secure Remote Password protocol parameters (which cannot be used to access your account). Tokens present in certain emailed links for invitations, provisioning and account recovery and other non-secret account metadata relative to our internal architecture (e.g. version numeric identifiers, file numeric identifiers). In the infamous words of Prime Minister Tony Abbott - shit happens.

Ladybird browser engine reaches alpha stage

Remember the Ladybird web browser? It's a project led by Andreas Kling, who left working on SerenityOS to create an all-new, from scratch, web browser engine. There's now an alpha version for Mac and Linux for people to try out. Apparently it works well considering the immense task of building a web browser engine that can support modern websites. The Ladybird team wants people to go nuts trying to break it, hack it, use it, etc, so they can get to it to a point where it's good enough for daily use one day. I would be remiss not to mention that the Ladybird project is fascist adjacent - the project is partly funded by some not very nice people and Andreas is sympathetic to some of those fascist-esque views. It's a shame there's that stink around Ladybird, because what they've achieved with the web browser engine is impressive.
