“The biggest thing since ChatGPT”

I should be the target audience for a personal AI assistant. I make videos, write, build software, and run a business. There should be plenty to hand over.

But how much automation do I actually need in my life?

That’s the question I kept coming back to while trying Meta’s Muse. There are things I like about it. Having one assistant to talk to makes sense to me. Having several little characters sending notifications and waiting for instructions gets confusing pretty quickly (Grok Bot). Still, even a well-designed assistant needs a job I would otherwise have to do.

I don’t want to spend my afternoon inventing chores for software.

The video covers my first encounter. This report looks at what Meta has shipped, what its privacy claims mean, and which tasks seem sensible to try. It also gives you a way to measure whether those tasks actually save time after setup, corrections, and signing in again.

Maybe you find one recurring job that helps. Maybe you decide your existing tools are enough. Both are useful outcomes.

What Meta has shipped

Meta announced Muse on September 8, 2026. It is a personal agent powered by Muse Spark, with a dedicated virtual computer and browser. The launch covers the United States through iOS, Android, and the web. You can also message it through WhatsApp; support for Meta’s AI glasses is described as coming soon. Muse is distinct from the underlying model and from Muse Code, the coding product. Meta’s launch announcement.

That distinction helps explain the failures you might encounter. A capable model can still be stuck behind a broken sign-in, a restricted preview, or an unavailable integration. Changing the wording of a prompt won’t necessarily fix any of those.

Meta’s product FAQ confirms a limited free tier, with the option to wait for usage to refresh or subscribe for more. Launch coverage reports Power at $20 a month and Maximum at $100. I couldn’t verify a complete public, account-independent allowance table from Meta’s accessible pages, so treat the checkout and usage screen in your account as the place to confirm the actual offer. Meta’s product FAQ, TechRadar’s September 14 hands-on report.

A token allowance doesn’t translate neatly into a number of completed tasks. An agent may make many model calls while reading pages, deciding what to do, and checking results. A difficult task can consume substantial usage and still fail. For a trial, record both the usage change and the result you accepted.

Before subscribing, ask yourself what happens when you hit the limit: does a running task pause, when does the allowance reset, and can another charge occur? Confirm those details in the plan shown to you. There is little reason to pay for capacity before you have a recurring use for the free allocation.

One assistant can still do several things

Meta describes Muse as a main conversation with optional side chats, persistent memory, background work, goals, and editable memory files. The design team says it chose chat bubbles partly because messages can arrive out of order while multiple tasks are underway. It also says early testers struggled to know where to start, which led to proactive suggestions. Meta’s design account.

I like the idea of having one place to ask for an update. That doesn’t require the software underneath to perform every step sequentially. There can be several processes working on a request while you deal with one assistant.

There are technical reasons to divide work. Anthropic’s research engineering team describes giving subagents separate context and independent research directions. It also reports substantially greater token consumption for its multi-agent research system. Those are findings about its own system, not a measurement of Muse, but they illustrate why parallel work can improve coverage while consuming more resources. Anthropic’s engineering report.

For the user, the useful question is who keeps track of the result. If three tasks are running, I want to know which are done, which need a decision, and which are blocked. I don’t need three personalities to explain it.

This is a reasonable instruction to try:

Keep a short task register for the work I give you:
task | requested result | status | next action | needs me?

Use these statuses: running, waiting for me, blocked, completed.
Mark work completed only after checking the requested result.

Keep routine progress in the register. Notify me when a result
is ready, a decision is necessary, or a deadline is at risk.
Combine nonurgent updates into one message.

When I give you an unrelated task, preserve the existing work.
If you cannot keep both moving, explain which one will wait.

This is a proposed operating preference. It doesn’t add scheduling or task-management features the product lacks. Check that Muse follows it before relying on the register.

Read the privacy claims carefully

Meta’s security description makes four distinctions:

QuestionWhat Meta documentsCan the working agent see actual credentials?Secrets are kept outside its runtime; approved requests receive credentials through a separate mechanism.Can the agent grant itself permission?A separate Sentinel service controls connector actions and outgoing network requests.Can Meta access the current VM?The launch architecture restricts access through operational policies; it does not technically prevent necessary Meta access.Is there a stronger private mode?Confidential VM is planned for later in 2026, with the aim of verifiably preventing Meta access.

Meta also says its email connector filters login codes and reset links, and that prompt injection remains unresolved. These are the company’s architectural claims, not an independent audit performed for this report. Meta’s security documentation.

WIRED reports that Moxie Marlinspike is working with Meta on Confidential VM. That collaboration should not be presented as proof that the stronger privacy mode is already available. WIRED’s reporting.

Training is a separate setting. Muse’s terms say you can opt out of using your content to improve Meta’s AI through Settings under Data Controls. They also state that you must be at least 18, subject to any higher applicable age requirement. Muse’s supplemental terms, sections 1 and 2.

Meta says Muse conversations and VM data are not shared with its advertising systems. That is a specific commitment, and it shouldn’t be expanded into a claim that everything you do through an agent becomes invisible to the websites you visit. Meta’s launch announcement.

My practical preference is to connect one service for one task first. A publication report needs access to the publication’s numbers. It doesn’t need permission to publish a post, change prices, or contact subscribers.

Set the permissions in the interface wherever controls are available. A chat instruction is useful for explaining intent, but it should agree with the permissions you’ve granted. Review both when expanding the task.

Also separate a lost login from a security failure. Being signed out is inconvenient. It doesn’t, by itself, demonstrate that credentials were exposed or that the user signed in incorrectly. Check the session before deciding what caused the problem.

Decide whether the task deserves an agent

Start with something you already do or repeatedly avoid doing. Write down the last three occasions it came up. If you can’t remember any, it probably isn’t the first automation to build.

I’d evaluate a candidate with five questions:

  1. How often does it happen?

  2. How much of my attention does it take?

  3. How easily can I verify the result?

  4. How disruptive would an incorrect action be?

  5. Does delegation require more access than I’m comfortable giving?

A weekly report with five numbers is easy to check against a dashboard. Negotiating an open-ended dispute on your behalf is harder to evaluate and gives the agent more opportunities to misunderstand your preferences. Both may be technically possible; the report is a more manageable first experiment.

Use this calculation over a fixed period:

Attention saved = manual time avoided
                 - setup time
                 - prompting and approval time
                 - checking and repair time

Suppose a weekly report takes 15 minutes by hand. Over four weeks that’s 60 minutes. If setup takes 20 minutes and reviewing each automated report takes 4 minutes, you save 24 minutes in the first month. If each run also needs a 7-minute login repair, you spend 4 minutes more than the manual process.

Those are illustrative numbers. They show why a fast first response can coexist with an unhelpful workflow.

Track elapsed time separately. An agent may take 30 minutes while you do something else, but require only 2 minutes of attention. That’s different from an agent that finishes in 5 minutes while asking you to intervene repeatedly. Deadlines still matter; use both measurements.

For an occasional task, a checklist or calendar reminder may be sufficient. For a recurring task with changing inputs and a result you can inspect quickly, an agent becomes more interesting.

The workflows below are proposed templates, inferred from the use cases in the transcript and the researched capabilities. They have not been run end to end in Muse for this report. Each includes a small acceptance check so you can find out what works in your account.

Workflow one: a publication report you can verify

“How many subscribers did I gain yesterday?” sounds precise until you try to define it. Does it mean new free subscriptions, paid upgrades, or net audience growth after departures? Which timezone defines yesterday? Are you looking at subscriptions or followers?

Substack distributes information across Home, Posts, Stats, and subscriber views. Its documentation distinguishes publication-level measures from individual post performance. Use the underlying pages to verify the metric instead of accepting a number whose label sounds close enough. Substack’s metrics guide, Substack’s Posts guide.

Start with one completed date and a publication you can check yourself:

Create a read-only report for [publication] for [YYYY-MM-DD],
using America/Chicago as the reporting timezone.

First verify the signed-in publication and the date range shown.
If the dashboard uses another timezone, report that limitation.

Report, when the dashboard provides them:
- New free subscriptions.
- New paid subscriptions or upgrades, preserving its labels.
- Departures or cancellations, preserving its definitions.
- Net change, only if it is directly shown or can be calculated
  from compatible measures.

For each number, give the exact metric label, source page,
date range, and collection time. Missing data is unknown, not zero.
Do not substitute followers for subscribers.

Also inspect the latest published post's available audience and
delivery information. Distinguish who can read the post from
who was sent an email. If one is unavailable, say so.

Do not publish, edit, email anyone, export subscriber identities,
or change account settings. Return a compact table.

Compare the result with the dashboard. Then run it for another date. Check the account, period, and labels again; a plausible number from the wrong period is still wrong.

For a recurring version, request a weekly summary after your reporting week closes. Preserve the last successful report and show the observation date. If access fails, the new report should say it failed rather than reuse old values as if they were current.

The acceptance condition is straightforward: every reported number can be located or reconstructed from the named source, and you can review the report faster than producing it yourself.

Workflow two: alerts that don’t become another feed

A personalized feed can be entertaining. I would treat that separately from a monitor intended to save time. For a monitor, specify what change would make you act.

Good candidates include a particular item’s availability, a deadline for an event you plan to attend, or a change on a competitor’s pricing page. “Keep me informed about AI” provides very little guidance about when to interrupt you.

Try one public page first:

Monitor [URL] for [specific condition].

Create a dated baseline first. Identify the exact item, plan,
region, currency, or event so similarly named options don't match.

Check at [frequency and timezone] until [end date]. If scheduling
is available, show the saved schedule and its next run. Otherwise
tell me you cannot schedule it; don't imply it will run later.

Alert me only when [condition]. Include the old value, new value,
source link, and observation time. Verify the source again before
sending an alert. Suppress repeated alerts for the same change.

If access fails twice consecutively, send one failure notice.
Do not treat a failed check as evidence that nothing changed.
Do not purchase, register, or contact anyone.

For a price monitor, define the variant and the total you care about. A lower advertised price for the wrong size is irrelevant. Shipping, availability, and membership requirements can also change the decision. Have the agent present whatever it can verify and label the rest unknown.

Before scheduling, ask it to classify three examples: a qualifying change, an irrelevant change, and an inaccessible page. This checks whether your instructions are understood, though it doesn’t establish that the live monitor will work.

After the first scheduled run, inspect the recorded outcome. The acceptance condition is a verified, actionable alert or a recorded successful check. A promise to keep watching is insufficient.

Workflow three: household coordination with a clear owner

Meta’s design team describes using Muse for school messages, supplies, dates, and a time-sensitive sports registration. That’s a company-authored example, not independent evidence of typical performance. It does suggest a more concrete audience than “anyone who uses AI”: a household juggling information across several places. Meta’s design account.

If this is your situation, begin with a narrow planning task. Ask for a weekly list of obligations from material you’ve selected. Expand access only if gathering that material becomes the main inconvenience.

From [selected messages/documents/calendar], prepare next week's
household action list for [date range and timezone].

For each item include:
action | responsible person | due date | source | unresolved detail.

Separate confirmed commitments from suggestions. If two sources
disagree, show the conflict. Do not guess a date or assign a person
who hasn't agreed to take responsibility.

Group purchases into a proposed shopping list. Group possible
calendar changes into a separate list for review.

Do not send messages, submit forms, buy items, or change the
calendar. Give me one concise review, ordered by deadline.

Use this once against a week you already understand. Deliberately include one changed date or cancellation and check that it isn’t reported as an active obligation.

If you later permit calendar edits, require the proposal to show the title, date, time, timezone, participants, and source. Have it check for an existing event before adding another. For purchases, review the actual item, quantity, delivery estimate, and total.

The potential benefit is less time collecting details. Deciding who will drive, whether to attend, and what the family wants may still belong in a conversation between people.

Workflow four: build a decision tool that works for other people

A page that lets someone rank choices is easy to imagine. A shared vote requires additional decisions: where votes are stored, how participants are identified, whether they can change their answers, and who can see the results.

Browser-local storage persists data within a browser’s storage for an origin; it doesn’t provide a shared tally across your friends’ devices. Also, a sandboxed iframe without allow-forms can display a form whose submission is blocked. These are different problems, and both can affect a generated tool. MDN’s Web Storage documentation, MDN’s iframe documentation.

A backend can be reached through an ordinary web link. The question is whether the builder offers suitable hosting and shared storage. There is no general rule that a server-backed app cannot be shared by URL.

Choose the simplest deliverable that serves your group:

For a shared poll, try this brief:

Build a private game-night poll for [group size].
Each participant ranks up to three games from [list].
Scores are 3, 2, and 1 point. Show ties explicitly.

Before building, identify available hosting and shared storage.
Explain how participants are identified and who can access votes.
Do not substitute independent local copies for a shared tally.

Test two separate participant sessions. Submit different ballots,
reload, and confirm both sessions see the same totals. Change
one ballot and verify the old ballot is replaced, not duplicated.
Verify the organizer's link works outside the creator's session.

If the environment cannot support this, propose a clearly labeled
copy-and-paste ballot formatter or an existing polling service.
Wait for my choice before changing the deliverable.

For a casual group, a basic poll may be entirely sufficient. Don’t add accounts, elaborate scoring, or a dashboard unless they solve a problem the group has.

If Muse produces a broken artifact, report the failed action and the observed error. Ask which restrictions belong to the hosting environment and which belong to its code. A generated page may not control the parent frame’s permissions. The repair might be a supported interaction, a different artifact format, or another host.

Acceptance means using the link as another participant. Looking at a finished preview is only part of the check.

Workflow five: a production packet before generated media

“Make a podcast” can mean several things: plan episodes, organize recordings, edit audio, synthesize hosts, or publish a feed. “Make a video” leaves similar gaps. Define the output before asking for production.

For a creator with an existing voice, I would start with preparation:

Create a production packet for [show] about [topic].

Audience: [who watches/listens].
Purpose: [what they should understand].
Source material: [approved links, transcript, and notes].
Target duration: [minutes].

Deliver a short premise, a segment outline, factual claims with
sources, unresolved questions, and suggested visual examples.
Use [writing samples] for tone without inventing experiences.

Keep quotations separate from paraphrases. Label speculation.
Do not generate synthetic hosts, imitate my voice, create an RSS
feed, publish, or schedule recurring episodes.

Draft one representative segment before expanding the script.

For a deliberately synthetic comedy clip, use a different brief:

Write a [duration]-second satirical script about [public product].
The premise is [specific absurdity]. Use fictional characters.
Make the joke through dialogue and concrete situations.

Keep factual product statements in a separate source checklist.
Do not invent allegations about identifiable people.
Make it clear which lines are exaggerated for comedy.

Give me the script and a shot list first. After approval, create
a rough cut using the media tools actually available. Identify
missing capabilities before promising a finished format.

G-Stack provides a good example of why the factual checklist matters. Its repository describes workflow skills and browser tooling for development tasks. You can make fun of the presentation while still explaining what the software provides. A joke that nobody understands it doesn’t establish that it has no function. G-Stack’s repository.

Evaluate the writing before spending more time on rendering. Does the first sentence establish the premise? Does each scene add something? Would the joke survive if you removed the product name? Then evaluate the media separately: speech clarity, timing, captions, image quality, and export format.

A finished audio file is also distinct from a working podcast feed. If distribution is part of your request, include a separate check that the intended feed receives the episode and that a listener can play it. Otherwise, stop at the file you requested.

Workflow six: convert a business ambition into an experiment

A revenue goal can produce an impressive-looking roadmap without resolving the uncertainty in the business. I would use an agent to organize evidence, calculate scenarios, and maintain follow-up work.

Begin with a short baseline: current customers, current recurring revenue, actual prices, recent growth, churn if measured, and the constraints on your time. Mark missing numbers as missing. Keep one-time sales separate from recurring revenue.

For illustration, at $20 per month per customer, $1 billion in annual recurring revenue corresponds to about 4.17 million paying customers. That’s arithmetic assuming a constant price and no discounts; it says nothing about whether you can acquire or retain them.

A useful next question is what you need to learn this month:

Use the attached business baseline to propose a 30-day experiment.

Start by separating measured facts, assumptions, and missing data.
Identify one uncertainty that could materially change the plan:
customer demand, activation, retention, pricing, or distribution.

Propose three small experiments. For each, state the hypothesis,
required work, expense limit, observed metric, and decision rule.
Explain what a negative result would mean.

Recommend one experiment that fits [available time and budget].
Draft the materials for review. Do not contact customers, publish,
change pricing, or spend money.

Set out what evidence we should collect and when we should decide
whether to continue, change the experiment, or stop.

An activation experiment might test whether new users can reach a useful first result without help. A distribution experiment might test whether a particular audience responds to a concrete offer. The agent can draft materials and summarize results. It cannot make weak evidence stronger by adding more milestones.

The acceptance condition is an executable experiment whose outcome changes a decision. A long document listing things a company could do doesn’t meet that condition.

Use a bounded recovery instruction:

The requested result is [result]. I observed [failure].

Inspect the current state. Explain the most likely cause and
the evidence for it. Label other possible causes as hypotheses.

Try one repair within the existing permissions. Check the result.
If it still fails, stop and report the blocker, the work preserved,
and the smallest action needed from me.

Do not repeat an external submission unless you have checked
whether the first attempt already succeeded.

The last instruction is useful whenever a timeout leaves an uncertain outcome. Before retrying a booking or message, establish whether it already exists. “I didn’t receive confirmation” and “the action didn’t happen” are different observations.

Maintain a small handoff note for recurring work: the requested result, source, last successful run, output location, and known blocker. Keep passwords and login codes out of that note. If continuity fails, the note should let you resume without reconstructing the entire conversation.

Does this become a mass-market product?

I don’t know. My own lack of recurring tasks is a meaningful reason for me to use an assistant less. It isn’t a survey of everybody else’s life.

A parent coordinating several schedules, someone managing a move, or a person who finds website interaction difficult may have considerably more to delegate than I do. That is a hypothesis about useful audiences. The next step is observing whether those people get reliable results with less effort.

The competitive question also needs to account for products that already exist. OpenAI’s current documentation describes ChatGPT Work using its own cloud browser, signing into sites, and continuing after the user steps away. So browser-based delegation inside ChatGPT is already part of the comparison, rather than a future possibility. Availability and sign-in support can vary. Official OpenAI browser documentation.

Muse therefore needs to be judged on the experience of using it repeatedly: setup, access, recovery, interruptions, useful context, and completed tasks. A list of capabilities alone won’t tell you which product you’d keep.

Zuckerberg argues that labs can make their own safety decisions and says Meta delayed Muse’s release. Associated Press reporting on the statement.

That leaves a separate question about the limits of those incentives. A delayed release doesn’t establish that every safety concern was merely a software bug. Nor does it establish that market incentives resolve every risk. Those broader claims would need their own evidence.

For this report, the more useful question is whether the product takes a task off your list without creating a longer list of things to supervise.

Choose one recurring task and one occasional task. Keep the recurring task small enough to run several times during the week.

A week is a small sample. Several successful runs don’t guarantee reliability on every website or future input. They do give you a better basis for a decision than the first impressive artifact.

Keep the task if it produces something you need and reduces your effort. Narrow it if the useful portion works but the rest needs supervision. Remove it if you’re maintaining an automation you don’t use.

I’d rather have one report I trust and an alert I occasionally act on than a busy assistant finding reasons to message me.
